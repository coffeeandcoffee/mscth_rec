\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This thesis investigates whether consumer-grade Electroencephalography (EEG) can predict when a user is actively sustaining cognitive engagement during naturalistic TikTok browsing. Using a Muse S headband (4 channels, 256~Hz), we recorded brain activity from 25 participants while they freely browsed their personal TikTok feed without artificial constraints. Behavior was mapped via tracking video transitions (screen swipes), allowing us to construct a rigorous binary classification framework focused on the underlying cognitive state: \textit{sustained engagement} (\texttt{STAY}, Class~1) versus \textit{imminent task abandonment} (\texttt{SKIP}, Class~0), captured via the 3-second pre-skip window.

We implemented a robust pipeline applying baseline-normalized relative spectral power features. A Random Forest classifier (200 trees, 112 frequency-domain features) was evaluated under a blocked temporal 5-fold cross-validation protocol---designed to eliminate autocorrelation between train and test sets---yielding a mean intra-subject accuracy of \textbf{53.8\%} (Wilcoxon $p=0.0015$ vs.\ chance), with 20 of 25 participants exceeding the 50\% baseline. Three participants demonstrated strong individual classification (P12: 81.2\%, P7: 67.3\%, P22: 62.9\%).

Feature importance analysis revealed that predictive variance was distributed broadly across all seven frequency bands, with delta (17.5\%) and theta (14.8\%) capturing slightly more aggregate Gini importance than high-gamma (14.7\%), and no single band achieving statistical dominance. At the individual feature level, peak high-gamma values at frontal sites were the top-ranked predictors, suggesting the model captures transient high-frequency volatility within a whole-brain spectral pattern. An empirical ablation of the 50~Hz notch filter showed no significant performance change (Wilcoxon $p=0.76$), consistent with the interpretation that the 40--60~Hz range contains recoverable cognitive variance. The standard clinical Engagement Index ($\beta/(\alpha+\theta)$) failed to discriminate these states (52.3\% accuracy), confirming that full structural spectral representations are required. Cross-subject generalization via LOGO-CV collapsed to chance (49.2\%), confirming the idiosyncratic nature of the engagement signature.

These findings establish a proof-of-concept that consumer-grade EEG captures statistically significant, individualized cognitive engagement states during naturalistic algorithmic media consumption, while demonstrating that standard overlapping-window evaluation in BCI research can substantially inflate reported classification performance. Generalization to broader populations remains an open question requiring demographically diverse replication; the present contribution is the feasibility demonstration and methodological critique itself.

\chapter{Introduction}
Short-form video platforms like TikTok now fundamentally shape how billions of people allocate their attention, often in bursts lasting mere seconds. On these platforms, every single swipe represents a critical micro-decision: to stay and engage, or to skip and move on. Repeated hundreds of times per session, these sequential micro-decisions determine exactly what information people absorb, which health or educational messages effectively reach them, and what digital behaviors are continuously reinforced.

For public health communication, this dynamic poses a significant challenge. Health promotion interventions delivered via social media must compete head-to-head with highly optimized entertainment content for the exact same limited cognitive resources \cite{mccashin_using_2023}. Systematic review evidence confirms that TikTok has been adopted for a range of public health purposes, yet institutional accounts remain poorly engaged relative to personal creators precisely because they fail to leverage the platform's full attentional affordances \cite{mccashin_using_2023}. If a health promotion video fails to maintain engagement in the critical first few seconds, the core message never lands. To improve these interventions, we need to understand what actually causes a human brain to sustain engagement---not just what individuals retrospectively claim they liked, but rather what their objective neural state looked like during active, continuous viewing. This investigation will not help us determine wether a specific content-piece changes behaviour and why, but rather we investigate neural clues for potential behaviour change and other neural markers that may be psychiologically and behaviourally relevant for optimized behaviour change messaging in public health and beyond.

Understanding the neural signature of sustained engagement---and the exact moment that engagement begins to collapse---opens the door to designing communications that maintain attention precisely when the brain is most vulnerable to disengagement. This investigation is about understanding how to make health-relevant content as neurologically compelling as the content it competes against.

Specifically, this thesis asks: within a controlled proof-of-concept study, can consumer-grade EEG---using a 4-channel dry-electrode headband---reliably classify sustained cognitive engagement versus imminent disengagement during naturalistic TikTok browsing at the intra-subject level? As a secondary question, and with explicit acknowledgement of the sample's demographic homogeneity, this thesis further examines whether any such intra-subject neural signature shows preliminary evidence of cross-individual consistency.

\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Short-form video platforms such as TikTok structure user attention as a rapid sequence of binary micro-decisions: continue watching, or swipe away. Platform metrics and much of the surrounding public discourse treat continuous, uninterrupted viewing time as a proxy for user engagement, yet recent behavioral evidence suggests that prolonged, uninterrupted scrolling is at least as consistent with passive, dissociative consumption as with effortful, goal-directed attention. This thesis investigates whether electroencephalography (EEG), recorded unobtrusively during naturalistic short-form video browsing, can discriminate the neural state preceding a voluntary skip decision from the neural state during sustained, non-skip viewing, and whether any such discrimination exceeds what is achieved by the conventional Engagement Index ($\beta/(\alpha+\theta)$), a composite band-power ratio drawn from clinical and educational neuroscience.

Twenty-five participants ($n=25$; 13 female, 12 male; age $M=24.6$, $SD=2.9$) were recorded with a consumer-grade, four-channel Muse S headband (AF7, AF8, TP9, TP10) while freely browsing a personal TikTok ``For You'' feed for 25 minutes in a private, distraction-free setting, following a two-minute resting baseline. Manual swipe events were synchronized to the EEG stream via keypress markers. A literature-motivated labeling scheme operationalized two classes: \texttt{SKIP}, drawn from a 3.0-second imminent-skip period ending 2.0 seconds before each swipe (a gap exceeding both the reported onset of the Bereitschaftspotential and directly measured pre-movement EMG onset in comparable self-paced tasks), and \texttt{STAY}, comprising all remaining continuous, non-boundary viewing. From this labeling, 236 univariate features spanning seven frequency bands, four electrodes, and the raw broadband signal were engineered, including band power statistics, Hjorth parameters, and two novel time-domain frequency descriptors (Peak Frequency, Macro Frequency).

Class separability, quantified via Cohen's $d$, was consistently higher for individual frequency bands, electrodes, and statistical descriptors than for the Engagement Index; the single strongest feature, raw-signal standard deviation at TP10, exceeded the Engagement Index's separability by nearly tenfold. In logistic regression benchmarking against a coin-flip baseline, the Engagement Index achieved above-chance but class-asymmetric performance, detecting \texttt{STAY} states substantially better than \texttt{SKIP} states. Three candidate features identified through the Cohen's $d$ ranking — TP10 raw standard deviation, AF7 high-gamma mean, and AF8 delta peak frequency — each yielded statistically significant improvements over the Engagement Index in \texttt{SKIP} detection at both the intra-subject and inter-subject level, though absolute performance remained modest and, in the cross-participant setting, did not exceed the chance-equivalent reference. A secondary behavioral analysis further showed that swipes cluster into rapid, individually variable ``burst'' sequences rather than occurring as uniformly spaced discrete events.

These findings indicate that the conventional Engagement Index captures only a limited, asymmetric component of the neural signal associated with short-form video browsing, and that more granular, region- and frequency-specific EEG features carry additional, currently underexploited discriminative information about imminent disengagement. The thesis further contributes a fully reproducible, open-source recording and analysis pipeline for consumer-grade EEG in naturalistic digital-media settings, and discusses the neuroscientific, psychological, behavioral, and public-health implications of these results, while explicitly flagging electromyographic and line-noise contamination as unresolved confounds requiring artifact-controlled replication.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Short-form video platforms have become one of the dominant modes of digital media consumption worldwide, and TikTok in particular has popularized an interaction paradigm in which content is delivered as an effectively infinite, algorithmically curated stream, navigated by a single recurring behavioral unit: the swipe. Each swipe is the outward expression of a micro-decision — to continue watching, or to move on — repeated hundreds of times within a single session. Understanding what happens, neurally and behaviorally, in the seconds preceding this decision is of interest not only to platform design and human--computer interaction research, but increasingly to public health communication, which must compete for exactly this attentional currency when delivering health messages, educational content, or behavior-change interventions through the same channels.

The default proxy for engagement used both by platforms and by much of the surrounding research and policy discourse is duration: how long a user continues watching before swiping away. This proxy is intuitive and trivially measurable from platform telemetry, but its behavioral validity is contested. Attention research using cued-recall paradigms has shown that sustained attention is better characterized as a fluctuating, trial-by-trial internal state, decodable from prestimulus EEG activity, than as a fixed property that scales linearly with time-on-task \cite{debettencourt_sustained_2021}. More directly relevant to the present context, two recent studies interrogating social media use specifically have found that extended, uninterrupted engagement does not reliably track productive attentional states: spontaneous flow experiences are rarely attributed to social media use even during heavy use \cite{knierim_flow_2026}, and extended, uninterrupted scrolling sessions are strongly associated with normative dissociation — users becoming ``lost'' in the feed, unable to later recall what they consumed \cite{baughan_i_2022}. Taken together, this literature suggests that continuous non-skip viewing time is an ambiguous behavioral signal, equally consistent with effortful engagement and with passive, low-encoding consumption.

This ambiguity motivates a different empirical question, one this thesis takes as its central object of study: rather than assuming that duration indexes engagement, can the moment of disengagement itself — the decision to skip — be characterized directly at the level of the EEG signal, independent of any prior assumption about what continuous viewing means? If a decodable neural signature precedes the voluntary skip action, it would offer a behaviorally grounded, physiologically anchored complement to duration-based metrics, and a candidate target for evaluating whether existing composite engagement measures, such as the Engagement Index ($\beta/(\alpha+\theta)$) widely used in educational and human-factors neuroscience, actually capture the process relevant to short-form video interaction.

\section{Research Questions}

This thesis addresses three interrelated research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Separability:} Can EEG recorded during naturalistic, self-paced TikTok browsing be used to distinguish the period preceding a voluntary skip decision (\texttt{SKIP}) from continuous, non-skip viewing (\texttt{STAY}), using only a lightweight, consumer-grade, four-channel headband?
    \item \textbf{Benchmark comparison:} How does the class-separation and predictive performance of individual, granular EEG features compare against the conventional Engagement Index, both as a descriptive effect-size measure (Cohen's $d$) and as a predictive model (logistic regression), at both the intra-subject and inter-subject level?
    \item \textbf{Parameter sensitivity:} How sensitive is any observed separability to the specific temporal parameters — window length, pre-swipe safety gap, and imminent-skip period — used to define the \texttt{STAY}/\texttt{SKIP} labeling scheme itself?
\end{enumerate}

Answering these questions requires solving a chain of non-trivial methodological problems that do not have an established, off-the-shelf solution in the literature: a naturalistic recording protocol that preserves ecological validity while achieving millisecond-level behavioral synchronization; a principled, literature-motivated labeling scheme for a self-paced (rather than externally cued) behavioral event; a feature space broad enough to allow genuinely comparative evaluation against a composite clinical baseline; and a cross-validation and significance-testing framework robust to the strong temporal autocorrelation and severe class imbalance inherent to naturalistic swipe data. The bulk of this thesis is accordingly devoted not only to answering the three questions above, but to building and documenting a reusable methodological pipeline capable of doing so reproducibly.

\section{Approach}

To address these questions, twenty-eight participants were recruited, of whom twenty-five ($n=25$) were retained for analysis after exclusion of compromised or ineligible recordings (Section~\ref{sec:method_participants}). Each participant was recorded with a consumer-grade Muse S EEG headband while freely browsing their personal TikTok feed for 25 minutes in a private, isolated setting designed to elicit naturalistic viewing behavior, preceded by a two-minute resting baseline used for per-participant signal normalization (Sections~\ref{sec:method_data_acquisition}--\ref{sec:method_experimental_protocol}). Manual swipe events were synchronized to the continuous EEG stream via a custom keypress-logging architecture built on the Lab Streaming Layer protocol (Section~\ref{sec:method_universal_recording_pipeline}).

A three-parameter labeling scheme was then developed to derive \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} classes from this continuous, self-paced behavioral stream, with default parameter values grounded in the EEG window-length literature and in the Bereitschaftspotential and pre-movement EMG literature governing motor-preparatory contamination near the swipe action itself (Sections~\ref{sec:method_skipstay_labeling_strategy}--\ref{sec:op_stay_skip}). Because none of these parameters had established precedent for a self-paced, naturalistic swiping paradigm specifically, their influence on downstream class separability was additionally characterized through a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}) rather than assumed to be optimal by default.

From the resulting labeled dataset, a broad feature space of 236 univariate EEG descriptors was engineered per 1.0-second analysis window, spanning conventional band-power statistics, Hjorth parameters, and two novel time-domain frequency descriptors, alongside features drawn from the undecomposed raw broadband signal (Section~\ref{sec:method_feature_engineering}). Feature-level and category-level class separability was quantified via Cohen's $d$ and benchmarked directly against the Engagement Index (Section~\ref{sec:method_class_separation_metric}), and the strongest candidate features were subsequently evaluated as standalone logistic regression classifiers against both a chance baseline and the Engagement Index baseline, under both intra-subject (blocked temporal cross-validation) and inter-subject (leave-one-participant-out) evaluation, with Wilcoxon signed-rank testing used to assess statistical significance across the participant cohort (Sections~\ref{sec:method_baselines_and_logistic_regression}--\ref{sec:method_metrics_and_significance}).

\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes five principal contributions, each developed in detail in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A fully naturalistic, ecologically valid EEG experiment synchronizing consumer-grade EEG with free TikTok browsing behavior in $n=25$ participants, together with a reproducible experimental protocol (Section~\ref{sec:results_exp_learnings}).
    \item An open-source, reusable recording and analysis pipeline covering automated EEG acquisition, behavioral synchronization, artifact-aware preprocessing, feature engineering, and statistically rigorous model evaluation, transferable to related naturalistic EEG paradigms beyond the present study (Section~\ref{sec:results_pipeline}).
    \item A characterization of swipe-burst behavior, showing that skip events cluster into rapid, individually heterogeneous sequences rather than occurring as uniformly spaced discrete decisions (Section~\ref{sec:results_burst_analysis}).
    \item A direct, statistically grounded comparison between the conventional Engagement Index and granular, feature-level EEG descriptors in their ability to separate and predict \texttt{STAY} versus \texttt{SKIP} states, identifying specific features (raw TP10 variability, AF7 high-gamma activity, AF8 delta peak frequency) that outperform the Engagement Index despite the latter's established status as a clinical and educational benchmark (Sections~\ref{sec:results_engagement_index}--\ref{sec:results_model_performance}).
    \item A neuroscientific, psychological, and behavioral interpretation of these results, together with an explicit, appropriately scoped discussion of their implications for public health communication, digital media policy, and health message design, and of the artifact- and hardware-related confounds that limit the strength of any causal claim (Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Structure}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~2 (Literature and State of the Art) situates the present work within the literature on attention and engagement in short-form video, sampling-boundary handling and parameter sensitivity in EEG classification, class-separation metrics, time-domain frequency estimation, and EMG and line-noise contamination in consumer EEG. Chapter~3 (Materials and Methods) details the participant sample, data acquisition hardware and protocol, labeling strategy and its literature grounding, preprocessing pipeline, feature engineering, and the statistical framework used for model evaluation. Chapter~4 (Results and Discussion) reports the five contributions summarized above and closes with a discussion of their neuroscientific, psychological, behavioral, and applied implications, together with explicitly flagged limitations. The thesis concludes with a set of concrete directions for future research, spanning validation and replication, mechanistic artifact clarification, theoretical development of the engagement construct, methodological expansion, and application to digital public health.

In a paradigm where participants transition between engagement and disengagement within seconds, the alpha denominator cannot be assumed to behave classically --- which predicts, before any data is collected, that the EI will struggle to discriminate \texttt{STAY} from \texttt{SKIP} in this context.

Within the present paradigm, these four electrodes therefore cover the two cortical regions most theoretically implicated in the moment-to-moment appraisal of whether a video warrants continued attention.

\section{Signal Processing and High-Frequency Legitimacy}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Claim:} Baseline relative power normalization effectively cancels out idiosyncratic physiological noise such as skull thickness and basal resting voltage differences across subjects.\\
    \textit{Search Query:} \texttt{"EEG" "relative power" OR "baseline normalization" "individual differences"}
    
    \item \textbf{Claim:} High Gamma (40--60 Hz) activity carries genuine, vital neurological variance related to complex cognitive binding, rather than just being muscular or electrical noise. Bellos et al.\ establish in their systematic review that gamma waves (30--100 Hz) are associated with high levels of cognitive processing, perception, and attention, and with peak emotional experiences \cite{bellos_methods_2025}. At the systems neuroscience level, gamma-band synchronization has been proposed as the fundamental mechanism through which attentional selection is implemented in cortical circuits, with attended stimuli producing stronger and higher-frequency gamma responses \cite{fries_rhythms_2015}. \section{Engagement and Attention in short-form video platforms}
The behavioral status of continuous, uninterrupted viewing on short-form video platforms is contested in the literature. Attention research using cued recall paradigms has shown that sustained attention is a fluctuating internal state — trial-by-trial, moment-to-moment variation in an EEG-decodable prestimulus signal that predicts subsequent encoding — rather than a fixed property that scales with time-on-task \cite{debettencourt_sustained_2021}. This distinction matters because two recent HCI studies suggest that longer, uninterrupted engagement with social media specifically does not track this kind of productive sustained attention, and may in fact index its absence. In a five-day field study pairing app telemetry with unprompted daily reconstructions, Knierim et al. found that participants spontaneously attributed flow experiences to social media in only 2\% of reported instances, and that within-person increases in social media use — particularly passive, feed-based use — predicted fewer subsequent flow reports and lower momentary readiness \cite{knierim_flow_2026}. Complementarily, Baughan et al. deployed a custom Twitter client and found that extended, uninterrupted scrolling sessions were strongly associated with normative dissociation: users reported becoming "lost" in the feed, losing track of time, and later being unable to recall what they had consumed, with interface features that segmented or interrupted continuous viewing (custom lists, reading-history labels, time-limit prompts) significantly reducing self-reported dissociation \cite{baughan_i_2022}. Taken together, this body of work indicates that duration of continuous, non-skip viewing is an ambiguous behavioral signal: it is equally consistent with effortful, goal-directed engagement and with passive, dissociative consumption in which little of the viewed content is encoded. Our \texttt{STAY} class therefore should not be interpreted as a direct behavioral proxy for sustained cognitive engagement; rather, it captures continuous non-skip viewing time, a necessary but not sufficient behavioral correlate of engagement that the prior literature suggests may just as often reflect disengaged, dissociative viewing.

A further methodological question concerns whether duration-based behavioral proxies for engagement can be validated, or at least cross-checked, against physiological markers, and here the evidence is more cautionary than supportive. Recent work directly contrasting EEG spectral markers of sustained attention against markers of cognitive engagement in a naturalistic learning context found that established engagement-sensitive markers -- notably frontal midline theta -- failed to discriminate between a sustained-attention task and a cognitive-engagement task, whereas posterior beta, occipital alpha, and the classical $\beta/(\alpha+\theta)$ ratio did discriminate reliably \cite{de_carvalho_selection_2026}, though the authors themselves attribute this null result partly to a baseline confound (a post-effort baseline potentially saturated with residual fatigue) and a modest sample (n=15), rather than presenting it as an unqualified failure of frontal midline theta as an engagement marker. The same study further showed that these markers followed distinct, non-linear (U-shaped and inverted-U-shaped) trajectories over the course of a continuous naturalistic task, such that aggregating spectral power over an entire viewing period -- the same logic underlying a duration-based \texttt{STAY} label -- obscures exactly the real-time fluctuations that separate momentary attentional states from one another. Taken together with the behavioral literature reviewed above \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026, debettencourt_sustained_2021}, this indicates that no existing marker, behavioral or neural, has been shown to positively validate continuous non-skip viewing time as a proxy for sustained cognitive engagement; the available evidence instead consistently undermines duration as a proxy, whether via dissociative viewing states, the rarity of flow during passive consumption, the trial-by-trial (rather than duration-locked) nature of attentional fluctuation, or the failure of aggregate engagement markers to track task state. The present thesis therefore does not treat the \texttt{STAY} label as validated evidence of engagement; it instead adopts it as an explicit, literature-motivated behavioral operationalization, and treats the subsequent EEG analysis (Methods Sections~\ref{sec:method_feature_engineering}--\ref{sec:method_result_synthesis_pipeline}) as an independent empirical test of what, if anything, separates \texttt{STAY} from \texttt{SKIP} at the level of the signal -- rather than as a confirmation of the label's validity.

\section{Sampling Boundaries in EEG and physiological-signal classification}
Boundary handling during epoch labeling is a recurring but often understated methodological choice in EEG- and physiological-signal classification. Rather than allowing a fixed window to span two adjacent labeled events, Moontaha et al.~\cite{moontaha_online_2023} process incoming EEG in non-overlapping tumbling windows and assign a class label to a window only when it falls entirely within a single stimulus interval; windows that straddle the boundary between two labeled segments are excluded from training rather than assigned to either class. This design choice reflects a broader assumption in the field: because short-timescale physiological signals are treated as stationary only within narrow intervals, and because the true onset of a cognitive or behavioral event relative to its recorded label is inherently uncertain, epochs near event boundaries are a primary source of label noise. Excluding them is preferred over resolving the ambiguity through interpolation or majority-vote heuristics, since misassigned boundary epochs directly degrade class separability during training. For the present work, this precedent motivates treating boundary overlap between \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} not as an edge case to be smoothed over, but as a structural feature of swipe-event labeling that should be excluded outright, even at the cost of reduced sample count.

\section{Parameter Choice in EEG-classification}
Existing evidence on temporal parameter choice in EEG-based classification is concentrated on two variables -- window length and label latency -- and is generally obtained by varying one parameter at a time rather than jointly. Window lengths between 1 and 5 seconds have been tested on both a benchmark dataset (AMIGOS) and wearable-EEG recordings, with a general decline in F1-score as window length increased across affect dimensions, classifiers, and devices, and 1-second windows performing best \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. The effect of label latency has been isolated by artificially delaying the availability of ground-truth labels by the mean stimulus duration (86\,s) while holding the rest of the pipeline fixed: this alone reduced mean arousal F1-score by roughly 20 percentage points (from above 0.82 to 0.637), and left valence classification below the level reported as chance \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. In motor-imagery decoding, time window and frequency bandwidth had previously been optimized individually but never jointly; addressing this with a full grid search across five time windows and 23 frequency bands showed both parameters to significantly affect classification accuracy (repeated-measures ANOVA, $p<0.001$ for each), while their interaction was nominally significant before correction ($p=0.044$) but did not survive the study's Bonferroni threshold, and the population-level optimal combination did not match the individually optimal combination for many participants \cite{mccartney_optimal_2026}. Read together, these studies indicate that temporal parameter choice can substantially affect EEG-classification performance on its own, that label latency in particular can be sufficient to collapse performance toward chance, and that one-at-a-time variation is the prevailing approach for characterizing these effects, notwithstanding its recognized inefficiency relative to designs drawn from statistical experimental theory \cite{saltelli_how_2010}. None of these studies, however, examine parameters governing the temporal placement of a label relative to a naturalistic, self-paced behavioral event rather than an externally imposed experimental cue -- the question addressed by the sensitivity analysis reported below.

Beyond the window-length and label-latency sweeps already discussed, two further strands of evidence bear on the direction of the effects examined in this thesis. First, on window length: Tzimourta et al.\ segmented EEG epochs into eight discrete lengths from 5\,s to 12\,s for the detection of Alzheimer's Disease and found that classification accuracy increased step-wise with window length across all six classification problems and five brain regions tested, with no reversals \cite{tzimourta_eeg_2019}. This provides an independent, monotonic precedent for the intuition that longer windows -- carrying more samples per estimate -- yield more separable features, in a paradigm unrelated to short-form video but sharing the underlying signal-processing logic. Second, on the placement of a measurement window relative to an upcoming event: Rimbert et al.\ varied the temporal position of the baseline window relative to the next motor-imagery trial and found that baselines positioned closer to the end of the preceding trial (i.e.\ further from the upcoming cue) produced significantly stronger, more spatially global event-related desynchronization and higher classification accuracy than baselines positioned closer to the cue \cite{rimbert_impact_2023}. This is structurally the same question as the gap-length and imminent-skip-period parameters examined here: both ask whether moving a labelled window further from a to-be-predicted motor event improves the separability of that label. Taken together, these two studies motivate the expectation that, in the present paradigm, larger windows and greater temporal distance from the swipe event should both improve class separability. However, this expectation should not be treated as a universal law of EEG parameter selection: Mi et al., sweeping window length for motor-imagery classification across nine subjects, found that the accuracy-optimal window size differed from subject to subject and was not monotonic within a single subject, with differences of up to 13\% between window choices \cite{mi_exploring_2026}. This indicates that window-length and event-distance effects, while directionally supported in the aggregate, can still be idiosyncratic at the individual level -- precisely the caveat carried forward into the parameter sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}, where gap length, window length, and imminent-skip period are swept one at a time and the resulting trends are reported descriptively rather than optimized into a final parameter choice.

This same boundary-handling logic directly informs the present pipeline's windowing procedure. Moontaha et al. process incoming EEG in non-overlapping tumbling windows and assign a class label to a window only when it falls entirely within a single stimulus interval, excluding windows that straddle the boundary between two labeled segments rather than resolving the ambiguity through interpolation or majority-vote heuristics \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. The present pipeline adopts the same principle at the point of window extraction: 1.0-second analysis windows are drawn with no overlap (stride equal to window length) specifically so that no window can span a class boundary, a safety gap, or an adjacent sample, mirroring the tumbling-window exclusion strategy used for stimulus-interval labels. Under this model, label noise concentrated at class boundaries is disproportionately harmful: it not only degrades classification accuracy but increases the number of training samples required to reach a given level of reliability, meaning that a smaller but purer set of boundary-excluded samples can outperform a larger set containing boundary-adjacent noise. Excluding boundary epochs is therefore preferred over resolving the ambiguity through interpolation or majority-vote heuristics, since misassigned boundary epochs directly degrade class separability during training, and the resulting loss in sample count is expected to be offset by the reduction in exactly the type of noise most costly to classifier performance. For the present work, this precedent motivates treating boundary overlap between \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} not as an edge case to be smoothed over, but as a structural feature of swipe-event labeling that should be excluded outright, even at the cost of reduced sample count.

\section{Sensitivity to Parameter Choice in EEG-based classification pipelines}
Methodologically, sensitivity to parameter choice in EEG-based classification pipelines is most commonly assessed through one-factor-at-a-time (OAT) variation, in which a single parameter is varied while all others are held fixed \cite{saltelli_global_2008}. Despite its widespread use, this approach is known to be statistically inefficient and cannot identify interaction effects between parameters, since any two OAT sweeps only ever probe the space along axis-aligned lines through a single fixed operating point \cite{saltelli_how_2010}. Nonetheless, OAT sweeps of individual temporal parameters have established precedent in EEG decoding: window length has been varied in isolation across a 1--5 s range while holding the remainder of the pipeline fixed, showing a general decline in classification performance as window length increases \cite{moontaha_online_2023}, and label latency has been varied in isolation by artificially delaying label availability, which alone reduced performance by roughly 20 percentage points \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. Where the joint effect of two parameters has instead been assessed via full factorial grid search, both parameters were found to significantly affect classification accuracy on their own, but their interaction did not survive correction for multiple comparisons \cite{mccartney_optimal_2026}, illustrating both the value and the limited scope of interaction analysis relative to OAT. No existing study, to our knowledge, applies OAT to parameters governing the temporal placement of a label relative to a self-paced behavioral event, as opposed to an externally cued or delay-based one; the gap length and imminent-skip period examined below extend the same logic of temporal-parameter sensitivity to this self-paced setting.

\section{Default Labeling Parameter Selection}
Choosing the temporal parameters that define the \texttt{STAY}/\texttt{SKIP} labeling scheme -- window length, gap length, and imminent-skip period -- requires grounding in existing evidence on EEG window sizing and on the timing of pre-movement neural activity, since these two literatures bear directly on two of the three parameters while the third has no comparable precedent. On window length, Moontaha et al.\ swept window sizes from 1 to 5~s on wearable-EEG affect classification and found a general, monotonic decline in F1-score as window length increased, with 1-second windows performing best across affect dimensions, classifiers, and devices \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. This motivates the present thesis's choice of a 1.0-second default window, rather than a longer window that would risk diluting the rapid, self-paced cognitive transitions this paradigm targets. On gap length, the pre-swipe exclusion window must be wide enough to exclude motor-preparatory activity from the \texttt{SKIP} class: the Bereitschaftspotential, the best-characterized EEG correlate of self-paced voluntary movement, begins as an early, slow-rising negativity approximately 1.5~s before movement onset, with a steeper late component emerging 400--500~ms before onset \cite{lew_detection_2012, shakeel_review_2015}. A 2.0-second gap therefore exceeds the full reported extent of the Bereitschaftspotential with a safety margin, ensuring that windows labeled \texttt{SKIP} reflect pre-decision cognitive state rather than the motor execution of the swipe itself. No comparable literature exists, however, for the imminent-skip period specifically in the context of a self-paced, naturalistic swiping paradigm; unlike window length and gap length, this parameter cannot be anchored to an established finding and is therefore treated in this thesis as a literature-motivated but ultimately heuristic starting value, addressed directly through the one-at-a-time parameter sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity} rather than through a fixed literature-derived justification.

\section{Class Separation Metric}
Quantifying how well a candidate label definition or feature separates two behavioral classes requires an effect-size measure that is standardized and scale-invariant, since raw mean differences are not comparable across features expressed in heterogeneous units (band power, frequency counts, raw voltage) or across labeling parameterizations that alter sample size. Cohen's \(d\), the standardized mean difference between two distributions expressed in pooled-standard-deviation units, was developed precisely to meet this requirement: by dividing the raw effect by the common within-population standard deviation, it yields a "pure," dimensionless index that is comparable regardless of the original measurement scale \cite{cohen_statistical_1988}. This property is what makes it suitable here, where the same separability question -- how distinguishable are two classes -- is asked repeatedly across quantities as different as raw broadband voltage, band-relative power, and peak-frequency counts. Cohen's \(d\) has established precedent specifically in EEG-based group- and class-separability analysis: Henao Isaza et al.\ use it to rank and select EEG features by the magnitude of the difference between an at-risk and a healthy control group prior to model training \cite{henao_isaza_comprehensive_2026}, and Kiarashi et al.\ use it to quantify how strongly a classifier's predicted probabilities separate a clinical from a non-clinical group, explicitly reporting its improvement as a marker of increased class discriminability \cite{kiarashi_sleep-derived_2025}. Both applications mirror the two uses made of the metric in the present work: ranking individual features by their standardized separation between \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} (Section~\ref{sec:method_feature_separation_cohen_d}), and tracking how that separation shifts under different labeling parameterizations (Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}). This precedent motivates treating Cohen's \(d\), rather than a raw or model-performance-based criterion, as the primary descriptive currency for separability throughout the labeling-strategy and feature-ranking pipeline.

Cohen’s \(d\) is the standard scale-invariant index for quantifying the standardized difference between two distributions, expressing a mean difference in units of pooled standard deviation rather than in the arbitrary units of the underlying measure \cite{cohen_statistical_1988}. This property is what makes it suitable for comparing separability across heterogeneous feature types — spectral power, entropy, connectivity, or raw amplitude — that would otherwise be incommensurable on their native scales. The metric has established precedent specifically in EEG-based group and class separability analysis: Henao Isaza et al.\ use Cohen’s \(d\) to rank EEG-derived spectral, connectivity, and entropy features by their ability to separate Alzheimer's-risk carriers from healthy controls, treating it as a principled feature-selection criterion prior to classifier training \cite{henao_isaza_comprehensive_2026}. Kiarashi et al.\ apply the same metric to quantify group separability between MCI and cognitively normal participants directly from model output distributions, and further show that separability under Cohen’s \(d\) responds systematically to methodological choices — in their case, multi-night signal aggregation — with (d) increasing alongside statistical significance as aggregation improved \cite{kiarashi_sleep-derived_2025}. Taken together, this precedent establishes two points relevant to the present work: first, that Cohen’s \(d\) is an appropriate and validated tool for ranking discriminative EEG features independent of their native units, motivating its use here for feature-level class-separation ranking (Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance}); and second, that Cohen’s \(d\) is equally appropriate as a sensitivity metric for tracking how a labeling or preprocessing parameter choice affects downstream separability, motivating its use here for the labeling-parameter sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}). Neither prior use, however, involves a self-paced behavioral labeling scheme comparable to the STAY/SKIP paradigm applied here — extending this precedent to that specific setting is a contribution of the present work.

\section{Peak Frequency as a Time-Domain Frequency Estimator}
Estimating a signal's frequency content directly in the time domain, without an intervening Fourier transform, has established precedent along two lines. Hjorth's Mobility parameter, one of the earliest and most widely adopted time-domain EEG descriptors, is defined as the square root of the ratio between the variance of a signal's first derivative and the variance of the signal itself, and is explicitly derived to correspond to the standard deviation of the power spectrum -- i.e., a formally grounded mean-frequency estimate obtained without computing that spectrum \cite{hjorth_contributions_1970}. A second, independent line of precedent is the zero-crossing rate, which counts sign changes per unit time and has established use as a time-domain frequency proxy in speech and EEG classification pipelines \cite{khambampati_frequency_2023}. Both methods are validated in the sense that their relationship to the underlying frequency content is derived analytically (Mobility, via spectral moments) or holds exactly for simple periodic signals (zero-crossing rate, for a pure sinusoid). Peak Frequency, as used in the present thesis, follows the same general premise -- that structural features of the raw time-domain signal encode frequency information -- but operationalizes it as a direct count of local maxima per unit time rather than a derivative-variance ratio or a sign-change rate. This specific operationalization has not, to our knowledge, been independently validated against ground-truth frequency in prior work; the closest analogous claim in the literature, that local extrema are informative of a signal's "global frequency" and function as a down-sampled representation of it, is asserted without independent validation in nonlinear time-series analysis \cite{niknazar_new_2018}. Peak Frequency is therefore treated in this thesis as a heuristic, self-defined descriptor motivated by this broader premise rather than as an established measure, and its discriminative value is instead established empirically via the feature-ranking ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance}.

\section{EMG Artifacts in EEG}
Temporal electrode sites are not neutral with respect to artifact risk: they sit directly over the temporalis muscle, one of the two dominant sources of myogenic contamination in scalp EEG, and this contamination is broadband, present even at rest, and not reliably removed by standard correction methods. Goncharova et al.\ characterized the spectral and topographical signature of this contamination directly, showing that temporalis contraction produces EMG with peak power at 40--80~Hz but measurable energy from 0 to over 200~Hz, that this activity is strongest at temporal and adjacent electrodes, and that even weak (15\% of maximum) contraction is sufficient to contaminate frequencies as low as 8~Hz \cite{goncharova_emg_2003}. Because EMG's beta-range peak power has been reported as low as 13--38~Hz at frontal and temporal sites specifically \cite{goncharova_emg_2003}, this contamination risk is not confined to high-gamma or the raw broadband signal alone; it plausibly extends to alpha- and beta-band features at AF7, AF8, TP9, and TP10 as well. Using a fundamentally different validation method---recording scalp EEG before and after complete pharmacological neuromuscular paralysis---Whitham et al.\ confirmed the magnitude of this effect directly rather than inferentially, finding 10- to 200-fold reductions in power above 20~Hz once muscle activity was eliminated, with the largest reductions at electrodes closest to temporal and cranial muscles \cite{whitham_scalp_2007}. Critically, this contamination is not contingent on deliberate muscle activity: using intramuscular single-motor-unit recordings simultaneous with EEG, Yilmaz et al.\ demonstrated that temporalis (and frontalis) motor units discharge tonically even when participants are instructed to rest, that this tonic activity produces detectable interference potentials on nearby EEG electrodes, and that this interference is significantly reduced but never fully eliminated by independent component analysis (ICA), the standard artifact-correction technique \cite{yilmaz_facial_2019}. This has a direct bearing on any interpretation of the broadband, unfiltered EEG signal specifically: because the undecomposed raw signal is not restricted to a conventional frequency band, it is structurally the signal component most exposed to this failure mode, since EMG's broadband character means no low-pass or band-limiting step can cleanly separate it from genuinely neural activity. The present thesis does not apply ICA or other artifact-correction pipelines to the raw broadband signal (Section~\ref{sec:method_universal_preprocessing_pipeline}), and does not deploy dedicated EMG reference channels of the kind used in more recent correction approaches \cite{li_electromyogram_2021}. Consequently, whenever the raw broadband signal or a temporal-site (TP9/TP10) feature emerges as strongly discriminative between \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} (Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance}), this literature motivates treating that result as ambiguous between a genuine neural signature and an artifact of jaw or temporalis muscle tension covarying with the skip decision, rather than treating it as evidence of one or the other by default. This ambiguity is addressed only descriptively in the present work (Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation}) and is flagged explicitly as a target for future artifact-controlled replication rather than resolved here.

\section{Delta-Band Function in Awake Cognition}
Although delta (0.5--4~Hz) oscillations are most extensively characterized during sleep, a separate and substantial literature establishes that delta oscillatory responses also play a functional role in awake human cognition. Güntekin and Başar's review of evoked and event-related delta responses concludes that delta activity is consistently involved in decision-making and attentional processes across a wide range of cognitive paradigms, and that reduced delta responses during cognitive tasks are a candidate general marker of impaired cognitive processing, observed across Alzheimer's disease, mild cognitive impairment, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and alcoholism \cite{guntekin_review_2016}. From a complementary evolutionary-functional perspective, Knyazev argues that delta oscillations index basic attentional and motivational salience-detection processes that are evolutionarily older than the higher-frequency (theta/alpha/beta) systems typically associated with more advanced, flexible cognitive control; critically, this account predicts that delta-linked processing becomes functionally more prominent precisely when these higher-frequency systems are less dominant or less effective \cite{knyazev_eeg_2012}. Taken together, this literature establishes two points relevant to the present work: first, that delta activity is not merely a sleep or pathology marker but a recognized correlate of decision-making and attention in the awake brain; and second, that delta's relative importance is expected to increase under conditions where composite, higher-frequency-weighted indices of engagement -- such as the classical Engagement Index, $\beta/(\alpha+\theta)$ -- fail to fully capture the relevant cognitive state. Neither line of work, however, has previously been applied to short-form video engagement or to a naturalistic, self-paced swiping paradigm; the only directly comparable evidence comes from short-form-video-addiction (SVA) research and is mixed rather than confirmatory. Xu et al., studying adolescents (mean age 12.3) during passive short-form video viewing, found reduced task-related delta engagement predicted worse working memory and cognitive control, though the effect was small and borderline-significant (F=2.184, p=.040, R\textsuperscript{2}=.115) \cite{xu_neural_2024}. Rahman et al., by contrast, found beta and gamma bands, not delta, to be the strongest predictors, though in a smaller sample (n=10) performing a coarser binary phone-use classification task (not a skip decision), using different gamma-band boundaries (30--44~Hz) than adopted here \cite{rahman_detection_2025}. Neither paradigm resembles the self-paced STAY/SKIP decision studied in this thesis, which is itself evidence that the present question has not previously been addressed rather than that prior evidence on it is merely mixed. This thesis therefore carries forward a specific, literature-motivated but non-committal assumption: that delta activity is a plausible candidate for carrying decision- and attention-relevant variance in the present naturalistic browsing paradigm, motivated by its established general role in awake cognitive attention and salience detection rather than by prior TikTok- or SVA-specific validation, which remains unsettled. Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance} tests this candidacy directly by evaluating delta-band separability against the Engagement Index and against other frequency bands, and Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation} returns to this literature in interpreting the resulting AF8 delta peak-frequency effect.

\subsection{Notch Filtering and the High-Gamma Band}
Standard practice for removing power-line interference from EEG is to apply a narrowband notch filter centered on the local mains frequency (50~Hz in most of Europe). This practice is uncontroversial when the frequency range of interest lies outside the notch, but becomes a substantive methodological choice when it does not: the conventional high-gamma band (40--60~Hz) straddles 50~Hz directly, meaning any notch filter wide enough to reliably suppress line noise necessarily attenuates a portion of the high-gamma spectrum alongside it. This is not merely a hypothetical concern for low-cost, dry-electrode systems specifically. Pattisapu and Ray, comparing a consumer-grade open-hardware amplifier (OpenBCI) against a research-grade system under identical conditions, found that the low-cost device exhibited pronounced mains-noise contamination together with additional intermodulation artifacts at frequencies flanking the line frequency (36~Hz and 64~Hz, symmetric around 50~Hz), an effect attributed to non-linear distortion introduced during amplification and not present in the research-grade comparison system \cite{pattisapu_stimulus-induced_2021}. It is worth noting this risk is not necessarily one-directional: the same comparison also found the consumer device to underestimate true gamma-band amplitude relative to the research-grade reference under matched conditions \cite{pattisapu_stimulus-induced_2021}, indicating hardware-related gamma distortion in this device class is not uniformly an inflationary artifact. Critically, this contamination pattern falls inside the same 40--60~Hz range this thesis treats as high-gamma, meaning that for consumer EEG hardware of the kind used here, the region a notch filter would need to suppress and the region carrying putative cognitive high-gamma signal are not cleanly separable in frequency. No published evidence, to our knowledge, quantifies how much genuine high-frequency cognitive variance a standard notch filter removes as a side effect in this hardware class, nor conversely how much of an un-notched high-gamma signal reflects line-noise and intermodulation artifact rather than cortical activity; the literature establishes that the risk exists in both directions without resolving it. Given this, this thesis treats the notched-versus-un-notched decision as an open methodological trade-off rather than a settled default, and elects to preserve the unfiltered signal for all reported analyses so that no frequency content is discarded on an unverified assumption (Section~\ref{sec:method_universal_preprocessing_pipeline}); the resulting risk that reported high-gamma and broadband findings partially reflect line-noise contamination is carried forward explicitly as a limitation in the interpretation of those results (Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation}).

\subsection{Pre-Motor Window and the Limits of the BP Literature}
The choice of a pre-swipe gap wide enough to separate a decision-related signal from the motor act that follows it rests on two distinct bodies of evidence, only one of which speaks directly to motor contamination. The first is the Bereitschaftspotential (BP) literature: a slow cortical negativity precedes self-paced voluntary movement, with an early component beginning approximately 1.5~s before onset and a steeper late component beginning 400--500~ms before onset \cite{shakeel_review_2015}. This literature is well established but answers a narrower question than it is often taken to answer -- it characterizes when neural \emph{preparation} for movement begins, not when the physical motor act itself, or its peripheral muscular correlate, begins. A gap defined solely by BP onset times therefore establishes that a window lies outside the window of neural movement preparation; it does not by itself establish that the window is free of motor execution or muscular artifact, since the BP and the muscular event it precedes are not the same thing and are not interchangeable justifications for a single design choice. Lew et al.\ close part of this gap directly: in a self-paced reaching paradigm structurally comparable to the present swipe task, arm muscular (EMG) activation was measured, rather than inferred from BP timing, and found to begin on average 263~$\pm$~40~ms before movement onset \cite{lew_detection_2012}. This is the only source in the present review that reports a directly measured, rather than literature-inferred, onset for the peripheral motor event itself. Taken together, these two findings support -- but do not by themselves prove -- the intuition motivating the present design: a gap of several hundred milliseconds to roughly 1.5~s should place a labeled window beyond both the neural preparatory signal (BP) and the measured onset of physical motor execution (EMG) in comparable self-paced tasks. This thesis adopts that intuition as the literature-motivated starting point for the 2.0-second pre-swipe gap (Section~\ref{sec:op_labeling_params}), while treating it explicitly as a margin derived from directly comparable but not identical paradigms -- reaching versus swiping, arm versus thumb -- rather than as a guarantee that the resulting \texttt{SKIP} window is free of motor-related signal. The task-specific validity of this assumption is not established by prior literature and is not tested independently in the present work; it is a boundary condition carried forward into the parameter sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}) rather than a settled fact.

\section{Labeling Strategy}
\label{sec:method_skipstay_labeling_strategy}
To quantify class separability under different labeling choices, Cohen’s \(d\) was computed as the standardized mean difference between the STAY and SKIP distributions. A one-at-a-time parameter sensitivity analysis was then performed in which gap length, window length, and imminent-skip period were each varied while the remaining two were held fixed (Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}).

\subsection{Operational Definition of Labeling Parameters}
\label{sec:op_labeling_params}
The labeling strategy was derived directly from the research questions and the aim of isolating neural states relevant to sustained engagement and imminent disengagement during naturalistic browsing. Accordingly, the computational target was constrained to two discrete classes: sustained engagement (\texttt{STAY}) and impending disengagement (\texttt{SKIP}), reflecting the distinction between sustained attentional maintenance, understood as a fluctuating internal state rather than a fixed behavioral duration \cite{debettencourt_sustained_2021}, and passive or dissociative browsing states in which continuous consumption need not indicate engagement \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026}.

The default labeling scheme was defined by three temporal parameters. The gap length was set to 2.0 seconds, the window length was set to 1.0 second, and the imminent-skip period was set to 3.0 seconds; a fixed 0.5-second post-skip orientation grace period was additionally excluded from both classes. These defaults were not chosen arbitrarily: the 1.0-second window matches the value found to maximize classification performance in a direct 1--5~s window-length sweep on wearable EEG, where accuracy declined monotonically as window length increased \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. The 2.0-second gap exceeds the onset of the early Bereitschaftspotential, reported at approximately 1.5~s before self-paced movement with a late, steeper component beginning 400--500~ms before onset \cite{lew_detection_2012, shakeel_review_2015}, so the gap was set beyond this window with a margin intended to reduce contamination of the \texttt{SKIP} epochs by motor-preparatory activity. This margin is further supported by directly measured EMG onset latency in a comparable self-paced reaching paradigm, where arm muscular activation began on average 263~$\pm$~40~ms before movement onset \cite{lew_detection_2012}, placing the 2.0-second gap well beyond both the neural (BP) and peripheral (EMG) onset windows reported in that study. The 3.0-second imminent-skip period was not derived from a specific literature value and is treated as an heuristic starting point, addressed directly by the parameter sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}. In the default configuration, the \texttt{SKIP} class therefore comprised the 1.0-second windows tiled across the 3.0-second imminent-skip period preceding the 2.0-second gap before each registered \texttt{A}-keypress (swipe event), yielding a label placement that aligned with the pre-skip attentional state while avoiding the most immediate motor-preparatory interval. The \texttt{STAY} class comprised all remaining continuous viewing segments that satisfied the exclusion criteria described below.

\subsection{Operational Definition of STAY and SKIP}
\label{sec:op_stay_skip}
The \texttt{SKIP} class was drawn from the imminent-skip period, defined as the 3.0-second interval terminating 2.0 seconds before the timestamp of each registered \texttt{A}-keypress (swipe event); the labeled \texttt{SKIP} epochs are the 1.0-second windows tiled within this interval. This interval captures neural activity preceding the physical act of skipping a video while placing the SKIP window beyond the motor-preparation interval, also known as Motor-Readiness-Potential or Bereitschaftspotential, which has been reported to occur approximately 1.5 s - 400 ms before movement onset \cite{lew_detection_2012}; the 2.0-second gap preceding the swipe marker therefore exceeds this interval, with limited margin at the earliest reported BP onsets. This gap also exceeds the directly measured onset of arm muscular (EMG) activation in a comparable self-paced task, reported at 263~$\pm$~40~ms before movement onset \cite{lew_detection_2012}, indicating that the gap is unlikely to be contaminated by the physical motor execution itself, though residual overlap with early BP onset cannot be fully excluded given between-subject variability.

The \texttt{STAY} class represents continuous, non-skip viewing periods, which are treated as a behavioral proxy for sustained engagement. It is noted that continuous viewing time is an imperfect proxy: passive dwell time can also reflect disengaged or dissociative states rather than effortful attention \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026}, and sustained attention itself is better understood as a fluctuating internal state than a fixed behavioral duration \cite{debettencourt_sustained_2021}. No EEG marker has been shown to positively validate duration-based labeling as a proxy for cognitive engagement; recent work directly comparing sustained-attention and engagement spectral markers found engagement-sensitive markers failed to discriminate task conditions and that duration-aggregated metrics obscure real-time fluctuations in cognitive state \cite{de_carvalho_selection_2026}, though the authors themselves attribute the frontal-midline-theta null result partly to a baseline confound and a modest sample (n=15) rather than to an unqualified failure of the marker. The \texttt{STAY} class was defined by extracting all remaining continuous viewing periods during the naturalistic TikTok consumption phases that were i) outside the 3.0-second imminent-skip period, ii) outside the 2-second pre-skip motor-preparation buffer, and iii) outside the 0.5-second post-skip orientation grace period.

\subsection{Baseline Exclusion}
The manually categorized pre-task resting baseline phases were excluded from the \texttt{STAY}/\texttt{SKIP} classification dataset and used only for per-participant signal normalization. Consequently, the algorithms were trained to discriminate between continuous non-skip viewing (\texttt{STAY}) and the period preceding a swipe-away event (\texttt{SKIP}) during browsing, rather than between resting and viewing. Figure~\ref{fig:labeling_strategy} illustrates the default labeling logic, including the press markers and the temporal placement of the labeling windows.

In addition to baseline exclusion, the labeling procedure omitted segments affected by boundary overlap between \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} definitions to preserve clear class assignment and avoid ambiguous temporal regions near each swipe event, following established practice of excluding windows that do not lie entirely within a single labeled interval \cite{moontaha_online_2023}. Boundary-adjacent mislabelling is known to be more probable and more consequential than uniformly distributed label noise \cite{frenay_classification_2014}, motivating this exclusion despite the resulting reduction in sample count. The final label construction therefore prioritized temporal purity of the target classes over maximal sample count.

\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}

To assess how label definition affects class separability, a controlled one-at-a-time (OAT) sensitivity analysis \cite[\S2.4.2]{saltelli_global_2008} over three temporal parameters was conducted: in each experiment two parameters were held constant while the third was varied, so that any change in separability is attributable to the varied parameter alone. This follows established practice in EEG decoding, where window length \cite{moontaha_online_2023} and label latency \cite{moontaha_online_2023} have been varied in isolation to quantify their effect on classification performance. Two limitations follow from the design. First, attribution is local to the operating point at which the remaining parameters were fixed \cite{saltelli_global_2008}. Second, interaction effects are not identifiable under an OAT scheme \cite{saltelli_how_2010}; resolving them would require a factorial sweep of the kind used for time window and frequency band in motor-imagery decoding \cite{mccartney_optimal_2026}. Independent evidence for monotonic window-length gains comes from Tzimourta et al., who found EEG-classification accuracy increased step-wise from 5\,s to 12\,s windows across six classification problems and five brain regions with no exceptions \cite{tzimourta_eeg_2019}. Similarly, in MI-BCI paradigms, baselines positioned further from the upcoming task cue yielded significantly stronger ERD and higher classification accuracy than baselines positioned closer to the cue \cite{rimbert_impact_2023}, mirroring the present gap-length and imminent-skip-period sensitivity. However, window-length effects are not always monotonic: a multi-scale CSP study found subject-specific optimal window sizes with accuracy differences of up to 13\% depending on window choice \cite{mi_exploring_2026}, reinforcing that the present sweep should be read as suggestive rather than as evidence of a universal optimum.

The key result is that, with fixed imminent-skip period and window size, increasing the gap length consistently increases Cohen’s d (Figure \ref{fig:window_exploration}, left), indicating greater separability as the SKIP interval moves further from the swipe event. A similar effect is observed for longer imminent-skip periods, where larger SKIP intervals also improve separability. For fixed gap and imminent-skip period, increasing window size is monotonically associated with larger Cohen’s d (Figure \ref{fig:window_exploration}, right), again corresponding to increased class separation. This pattern is consistent with independent findings in EEG classification more broadly: accuracy has been shown to increase monotonically with window length \cite{tzimourta_eeg_2019}, and separability has been shown to increase with distance between the measurement window and the upcoming event \cite{rimbert_impact_2023}. At the same time, this monotonicity is not universal across EEG paradigms, as optimal window length has also been shown to be subject-specific rather than fixed \cite{mi_exploring_2026}. While all these results strongly suggest better separability with increased overall data-availability (bigger windows, and areas) and simultaneously better separability the further we move away from the swipe, interpreting these relationships and their implications for optimal parameter selection remains beyond the scope of this thesis as it requires separate analysis, and is therefore a promising direction for future work.     \item Gap size (gs) = 2 seconds
    \item Imminent-skip period (isp) = 3 seconds
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Class Separation Metric}
\label{sec:method_class_separation_metric}
Cohen’s \(d\) is the standard scale-invariant index for quantifying the standardized difference between two distributions, expressing a mean difference in units of pooled standard deviation rather than in the arbitrary units of the underlying measure \cite{cohen_statistical_1988}. This property is what makes it suitable for comparing separability across heterogeneous feature types — spectral power, entropy, connectivity, or raw amplitude — that would otherwise be incommensurable on their native scales. The metric has established precedent specifically in EEG-based group and class separability analysis: Henao Isaza et al.\ use Cohen’s \(d\) to rank EEG-derived spectral, connectivity, and entropy features by their ability to separate Alzheimer's-risk carriers from healthy controls, treating it as a principled feature-selection criterion prior to classifier training \cite{henao_isaza_comprehensive_2026}. Kiarashi et al.\ apply the same metric to quantify group separability between MCI and cognitively normal participants directly from model output distributions, and further show that separability under Cohen’s \(d\) responds systematically to methodological choices — in their case, multi-night signal aggregation — with (d) increasing alongside statistical significance as aggregation improved \cite{kiarashi_sleep-derived_2025}. Taken together, this precedent establishes two points relevant to the present work: first, that Cohen’s \(d\) is an appropriate and validated tool for ranking discriminative EEG features independent of their native units, motivating its use here for feature-level class-separation ranking (Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance}); and second, that Cohen’s \(d\) is equally appropriate as a sensitivity metric for tracking how a labeling or preprocessing parameter choice affects downstream separability, motivating its use here for the labeling-parameter sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:method_parameter_sensitivity}). Neither prior use, however, involves a self-paced behavioral labeling scheme comparable to the STAY/SKIP paradigm applied here — extending this precedent to that specific setting is a contribution of the present work.

Cohen’s \(d\) expresses the standardized difference between the STAY and SKIP distributions, making it suitable for comparing separability across parameter settings even when the underlying distributions differ in scale. In this context, larger absolute values of Cohen’s \(d\) indicate stronger separation between the two classes.

\begin{equation}
    d = \frac{\bar{x}_{\text{STAY}} - \bar{x}_{\text{SKIP}}}{\sqrt{\frac{(n_{\text{STAY}} - 1)s_{\text{STAY}}^2 + (n_{\text{SKIP}} - 1)s_{\text{SKIP}}^2}{n_{\text{STAY}} + n_{\text{SKIP}} - 2}}}
    \label{eq:cohens_d}
\end{equation}
where \(\bar{x}_{\text{STAY}}\) and \(\bar{x}_{\text{SKIP}}\) denote the sample means of the feature values for the STAY and SKIP windows respectively, \(s_{\text{STAY}}^2\) and \(s_{\text{SKIP}}^2\) are the corresponding sample variances, and \(n_{\text{STAY}}\) and \(n_{\text{SKIP}}\) represent the number of samples in each class.

For each parameter configuration, Cohen’s \(d\) was computed independently between the STAY and SKIP distributions of every extracted univariate feature (e.g.\ \texttt{TP10\_raw\_std}), yielding one \(|d|\) value per feature. This provided a direct quantitative index of how strongly the chosen temporal definitions emphasized the contrast between the \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} behavioral classes. The two levels at which \(|d|\) is reported in the feature-ranking analyses---exact per-feature values versus category means---are described in Section~\ref{sec:method_feature_separation_cohen_d}.

\section{Universal Recording Pipeline}
\label{sec:method_universal_recording_pipeline}
To facilitate rigorous and reproducible data collection, a robust Python-based recording architecture (\url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_collection/recording_script.py}) was developed. This script orchestrates the simultaneous capture of raw EEG telemetry from the Muse S headband and has the capability of recording synchronized behavioral video from a webcam which was not used in this experiment, yet may be of value to future investigations. Its autonomous capabilities ensure that the experimental protocol can be executed reliably without extensive technical oversight.

For this study's recordings the command \verb|python recording_script.py --nocamera --duration 1800| was used to call the script. The two flags simply mean (i) bypassing camera recording, as it is not in the scope of this study yet kept for future investigations and (ii) the recording duration is 1800s = 30 minutes.
The pipeline's core features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Automated Hardware Discovery:} Upon launch, the script automatically scans the local Bluetooth environment for active Muse devices and enumerates all connected camera peripherals, allowing the researcher to select the optimal hardware interactively without requiring hardcoded MAC addresses or device indices.
    \item \textbf{Precision Synchronization and Event Logging:} A dedicated, threaded keypress listener (\texttt{pynput}) monitors the \texttt{A} and \texttt{B} keys used by participants to mark swipe events and baseline respectively. Crucially, the script computes a dynamic time offset between the system clock and the LSL stream clock. Keypresses are retroactively aligned to this LSL timeline within a defined tolerance window and embedded directly into the EEG data rows as binary flags, ensuring absolute temporal synchrony between cognitive events and physical interactions.
    \item \textbf{Simultaneous Multimodal Capture:} The script utilizes the \texttt{pylsl} library to interface with the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL), pulling raw EEG data from the Muse headband at 256 Hz. Unless deactivated with the flag, it concurrently employs OpenCV to capture and encode synchronized webcam video using the H.264 (avc1) codec, providing visual ground truth of participant behavior.
    \item \textbf{Resilient Auto-Reconnection:} To combat the inherently unstable nature of Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) connections, the script features an active connection-loss detection loop. If the LSL stream halts (no samples received within a timeout period), the script automatically terminates the hanging subprocess, rescans the Bluetooth environment, restarts the stream, and initiates a sequential data fragment (e.g., \verb|_2.csv|, \verb|_3.csv|). This guarantees that transient connection drops do not corrupt the entire recording session.
    \item \textbf{Concurrent Multi-Threading:} To ensure that blocking operations (such as writing high-resolution video frames to disk) do not disrupt the high-frequency polling required for EEG telemetry, the script isolates the LSL pulling loop, the OpenCV capture loop, and the keypress listener into independent Python daemon threads.
\end{itemize}

This open-source compatible architecture ensures that any independent researcher equipped with a standard webcam and a consumer-grade Muse S headband can fully replicate the data collection methodology utilized in this thesis.

\subsection{Data Synchronization and Interpolation}

To establish a uniform temporal grid suitable for across-session and across-participant analysis, the true sampling rate was derived by calculating the median time difference (\(\Delta t\)) between consecutive timestamps. Subsequently, a linear interpolation algorithm was applied to upsample and regularize the data to a constant sampling frequency of exactly \(f_s = 256\) Hz. This process guarantees that any subsequent spectral analysis relies on a strictly uniform time axis, avoiding mathematical artifacts stemming from temporal jitter. \subsection{Baseline Extraction and Normalization}
Following temporal synchronization, a baseline resting state was extracted for each participant to account for individual physiological differences, such as varying skull thickness or inherent resting cortical activity. The baseline period was rigidly defined as a 90-second continuous window commencing 10 seconds after the participant's initial \texttt{B} keypress (executed via \url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_analysis/step01_preprocess.py}). 

The interpolated signal was then separated into seven standard EEG frequency bands using a 4th-order Butterworth bandpass filter: Delta (1--4~Hz), Theta (4--8~Hz), Alpha (8--13~Hz), Beta (13--30~Hz), Low Gamma (30--40~Hz), High Gamma (40--60~Hz), and Very High Gamma (60--100~Hz). The undecomposed broadband signal was retained alongside these bands and is referred to throughout as the \emph{raw} signal. The instantaneous amplitude envelope \(A\) for each band was then obtained via the Hilbert transform. The linear envelope is used directly; it is not squared to power. 

To normalize the extracted features across participants, a z-score normalization was applied against the participant-specific baseline. For every temporal sample, the normalized band amplitude \(z\) was computed as:
\begin{equation}
    z = \frac{A - \mu_{base}}{\sigma_{base}}
    \label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_{base}\) and \(\sigma_{base}\) denote the mean and standard deviation of the band amplitude envelope derived exclusively from the aforementioned 90-second baseline period. This normalization ensures that all subsequent statistical evaluations and machine learning models operate on normalized deviations from a participant's own resting state, effectively mitigating absolute inter-subject voltage variance.

\subsection{Windowing and Class Labeling}
Following normalization, the continuous data streams were segmented and labeled into discrete \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} classes based on the participant's interaction events (\texttt{A} keypresses) as previously established in Sections~\ref{sec:op_labeling_params} and \ref{sec:op_stay_skip}. To strictly prevent motor artifact contamination arising from the physical swipe action, a \textbf{2.0-second gap} was enforced immediately preceding the \texttt{A} keypress, and a \textbf{0.5-second gap} was enforced immediately following it. No data was extracted from these gap periods.

The imminent-skip period was rigorously defined as the \textbf{3.0-second interval} immediately preceding the aforementioned 2.0-second pre-swipe gap, and all \texttt{SKIP} windows were drawn from within it (executed via \url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_analysis/step03_label_windows.py}). This temporal framing is intended to isolate the cognitive decision-making phase leading up to the swipe from the physical execution of the swipe itself; the 2.0-second gap exceeds both the reported onset of the Bereitschaftspotential (1.5~s--400~ms before movement) and the directly measured onset of arm muscular activation (263~$\pm$~40~ms before movement) reported in a comparable self-paced reaching paradigm \cite{lew_detection_2012}, though full elimination of motor-preparatory contamination cannot be guaranteed given individual variability in onset timing. The \texttt{STAY} class was defined as all remaining valid temporal periods outside of the \texttt{SKIP} windows and safety gaps.

Finally, discrete 1.0-second analysis windows were extracted using a sliding window with no overlap (stride = window length). This ensures that windows do not cross class boundaries, enter safety gaps, or overlap with adjacent samples, thereby preventing data leakage and artificially inflated model performance, consistent with the practice of excluding windows that do not lie entirely within a single labeled interval \cite{moontaha_online_2023}.

\subsection{Artifact Analysis}
To ensure the integrity of the analyzed EEG signals, an automated outlier detection approach was implemented to flag potential artifacts (executed via \url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_analysis/step02_artifact_flag.py}). The preprocessing pipeline was executed twice, once without and once with a 50~Hz notch filter. The artifact-detection procedure described in this section relies on peak-to-peak amplitude thresholding, a coarse, broadband measure targeting large-amplitude deflections from blinks and muscle activity; for this purpose only, the notch-filtered variant was used so that a constant, non-physiological line-noise component would not itself inflate the amplitude threshold. All analyses reported elsewhere in this thesis, including the frequency-band and gamma-range results in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}, instead use the un-notched data: because the line frequency sits inside the conventional high-gamma range (40--60~Hz), notch filtering is not a neutral operation, and this thesis prioritizes preserving the full, unaltered frequency content over pre-emptively discarding a band on the assumption that its content near 50~Hz is purely artifactual. Specifically, the 95th percentile of the peak-to-peak amplitude computed on the notch-filtered signal was utilized as a dynamic, participant-specific threshold. Frontal channels (AF7, AF8) were evaluated for ocular artifacts (e.g., blinks), while temporal channels (TP9, TP10) were evaluated for electromyogenic (EMG) artifacts, consistent with prior evidence that temporal electrode sites are disproportionately susceptible to EMG contamination \cite{goncharova_emg_2003, whitham_scalp_2007}; this susceptibility has been reported at frequencies as low as 13~Hz \cite{goncharova_emg_2003}, so it is treated here as a risk extending into alpha and beta bands, not only into high-gamma or broadband features. Absolute voltage thresholds (e.g., \(150\,\mu\mathrm{V}\)) were initially investigated but were ultimately discarded as they did not yield generalizable or useful results across the dataset.

No data points were excluded from the final machine learning pipeline based on these artifact flags. Incorporating such filtering would introduce additional experimental conditions and substantially expand the analysis beyond the scope of this proof-of-concept study. Instead, the approach is documented to highlight awareness of artifact-related confounds and to provide a foundation for more advanced rejection strategies in future work. \subsection{Engagement Index Calculation}
To provide a recognized clinical baseline for cognitive evaluation, the Engagement Index (EI) was calculated for each analysis window. The EI is theoretically grounded in established neurophysiological research correlating task engagement with frontal and parietal band power ratios. It is mathematically defined as:
\begin{equation}
    EI = \frac{\beta}{\alpha + \theta}
    \label{eq:formula_EI}
\end{equation}
In this pipeline, exactly one scalar Engagement Index (EI) is computed per \(1.0\)-second analysis window per participant. To calculate this single value, the within-window variance of the baseline-normalized band signal is first extracted for the Beta (\(\beta\)), Alpha (\(\alpha\)), and Theta (\(\theta\)) bands across each of the four individual electrodes. Because the normalized signal is z-scored and therefore centred near zero, the ratio is formed from variances rather than means; the variance is strictly non-negative, so the denominator cannot approach zero and the numerical instability of a mean-based ratio is avoided. These four spatial variances are then averaged together to produce a single, whole-head \(\beta\), \(\alpha\), and \(\theta\) scalar for that specific window, which are finally fed into the EI ratio formula. This single composite scalar serves as the primary clinical benchmark against which the predictive power of individual statistical features is evaluated in the machine learning phase. An example calculation of the EI for a single sample window of one participant can be observed in Figure~\ref{fig:calculation_visual_EI}, visually showing the three spatially-averaged scalars that are utilized in the Index calculation in Equation~\ref{eq:formula_EI}.

\subsection{Hjorth Parameters}
In addition to basic descriptive statistics, Hjorth Parameters (Activity, Mobility, and Complexity) were computed to capture the sequence-dependent temporal dynamics of the signal within the \(1.0\)-second window. While standard statistical moments characterize the amplitude distribution independently of their temporal order (i.e., they are time-invariant), Hjorth parameters depend explicitly on the time-domain derivatives, thus capturing spectral properties—such as mean frequency and bandwidth—directly from the time-domain signal.

For a discrete EEG signal vector \(y(t)\) of length \(N = 256\) representing one frequency band at one channel within a window, the three parameters are mathematically defined as follows:

\subsubsection{Activity}
Activity measures the total signal power and is defined simply as the variance of the signal amplitude:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Activity} = \sigma_{y}^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} (y(t) - \mu_y)^2
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_y\) is the mean of \(y(t)\). The input dimension is the normalized amplitude and the output dimension represents the squared amplitude.

\subsubsection{Mobility}
Mobility serves as a time-domain proxy for the mean frequency of the power spectrum. It is defined as the square root of the ratio of the variance of the first derivative \(y'(t)\) to the variance of the primary signal \(y(t)\):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Mobility} = \frac{\sigma_{y'}}{\sigma_{y}} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{var}(y'(t))}{\text{var}(y(t))}}
\end{equation}
Since the differentiation of a signal with respect to time yields a rate of change, the resulting output dimension of Mobility is expressed in Hertz (Hz).

\subsubsection{Complexity}
Complexity measures the bandwidth of the signal and indicates how closely the signal's shape resembles a pure sine wave (where a pure sine wave converges to a complexity of 1). It is defined as the ratio of the Mobility of the first derivative \(y'(t)\) to the Mobility of the primary signal \(y(t)\):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Complexity} = \frac{\text{Mobility}(y')}{\text{Mobility}(y)} = \frac{\sigma_{y''} / \sigma_{y'}}{\sigma_{y'} / \sigma_{y}}
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_{y''}\) is the standard deviation of the second derivative of the signal. Because it is a ratio of two frequency-domain (Hz) equivalents, Complexity is a dimensionless scalar.

Together, these three parameters output exactly three scalar features per frequency band per channel, expanding the univariate feature space by explicitly encoding the temporal structural variations that basic amplitude statistics fundamentally miss.

\subsection{Peak and Macro Frequency Extraction}
Beyond traditional amplitude statistics, two specialized time-domain features were engineered to capture the dominant oscillatory and structural patterns within each frequency band of the \(1.0\)-second analysis window. For a discrete band-pass filtered EEG signal vector \(y(t)\) of length \(N=256\) and total duration \(T = 1.0\text{ s}\), the features are calculated as follows:

\subsubsection{Peak Frequency}
Rather than extracting the maximum bin from a Fourier transform, Peak Frequency is calculated directly in the time domain by identifying the total number of local maxima (peaks) within the signal. A point \(y(t)\) is considered a peak if it is strictly greater than its immediate neighbors. Let \(P\) be the set of all peak indices. The Peak Frequency \(f_{peak}\) is defined as the absolute peak count normalized by the window duration:
\begin{equation}
    f_{peak} = \frac{|P|}{T}
\end{equation}
The input dimension is the time-series amplitude vector, and the output is a scalar frequency representing the unsmoothed oscillatory rate of the frequency band in Hertz (Hz). Peak-counting is conceptually related to, but distinct from, established validated time-domain frequency estimators: Hjorth's Mobility parameter derives a mean-frequency estimate from the variance ratio of a signal's first derivative to the signal itself \cite{hjorth_contributions_1970}, and the zero-crossing rate used in speech and EEG classification pipelines derives a frequency proxy from the rate of sign changes \cite{khambampati_frequency_2023}. Peak Frequency instead counts local maxima directly; this specific operationalization has no independent validation in the literature and is treated here as a heuristic descriptor rather than an established measure, addressed empirically via the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance}.

\subsubsection{Macro Frequency}
While Peak Frequency captures every local maximum, it is highly sensitive to noise and high-frequency jitter. To isolate the true, underlying macroscopic oscillatory bursts, a novel Macro Frequency algorithm was developed. 

First, a dynamic amplitude threshold \(\theta_{peak}\) is calculated as the mean amplitude of all previously identified peaks: 
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{peak} = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{p \in P} y(p)
\end{equation}
The signal is then converted into a binary sequence \(B(t)\) where \(B(t) = 1\) if \(y(t) \ge \theta_{peak}\) and \(0\) otherwise. 

Because a single macroscopic wave might contain slight amplitude dips that prematurely break a contiguous block of ones, the algorithm calculates the average length of all zero-sequences (gaps), denoted as \(\bar{G}\). Any gap in \(B(t)\) with a length \(g \le \bar{G}\) is mathematically bridged (converted to ones), producing a smoothed binary sequence \(B_{smooth}(t)\). 

Finally, the Macro Frequency \(f_{macro}\) is defined as the total number of distinct, contiguous blocks of ones in \(B_{smooth}(t)\), normalized by the window duration:
\begin{equation}
    f_{macro} = \frac{\text{BlockCount}(B_{smooth})}{T}
\end{equation}
The output is a stabilized, macroscopic frequency in Hertz (Hz) that robustly captures the count of dominant structural waves within the cognitive window, effectively ignoring micro-fluctuations.

\subsection{Raw Broadband Features}
The undecomposed raw broadband signal is not passed through the band-specific feature set described above; instead, a separate set of time-domain descriptors is extracted directly from the raw signal. Treating the raw signal as a distinct feature class is motivated by evidence that broadband/aperiodic activity can carry decodable information independent of, and sometimes exceeding, band-limited oscillatory features \cite{ovi_frequency_2026, donoghue_electrophysiological_2020}, and that band-power ratio measures can conflate periodic and aperiodic components \cite{donoghue_electrophysiological_2020}. Three time-domain descriptors are extracted per electrode: the mean, the standard deviation, and the linear slope of the signal across the window, the latter obtained as the gradient of a least-squares linear fit and expressed per second. Together these capture the amplitude level, the amplitude variability, and the direction and steepness of any sustained drift within the window.

\subsection{Resulting Feature Space}
Taken together, the two extraction schemes yield the final feature space. For each of the seven frequency bands at each of the four electrodes, eight statistics are computed---mean, standard deviation, relative power, the three Hjorth parameters, and the peak and macro frequencies---giving \(7 \times 4 \times 8 = 224\) features. For the raw broadband signal, three time-domain statistics are extracted at each electrode, giving a further \(1 \times 4 \times 3 = 12\) features. The complete feature space therefore comprises 236 univariate features per 1.0-second analysis window.

\subsection{Feature Separation Analysis}
\label{sec:method_feature_separation_cohen_d}
To mathematically evaluate the discriminative power of individual features prior to modeling, Cohen’s \(d\) was computed to measure the standardized effect size between the \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} distributions for each engineered feature (executed via \url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_analysis/viz17_feature_ranking.py}). Cohen’s \(d\) is computed as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:cohens_d}. As a standardized effect size, Cohen’s \(d\) provides a scale-invariant measure of class separation that is independent of the absolute units of the feature (e.g., microvolts versus power spectral density). It quantifies how many standard deviations separate the centers of the two classes, thereby capturing theoretical separability prior to any model training.

By calculating the absolute effect size \(|d|\), the pipeline systematically ranked all extracted features (e.g., TP10\_raw\_std) according to their individual ability to separate the \texttt{STAY} and \texttt{SKIP} classes. Aggregated plots grouped by frequency band, electrode, or statistic report the arithmetic mean of \(|d|\) across all constituent features in that category and are shown without confidence intervals; the band \(\times\) electrode profile is shown as a point plot with bootstrapped 95\% confidence intervals.

Confidence intervals are reported only for this band-electrode profile, where each point represents a single well-defined feature combination; category-aggregated means (by band, electrode, or statistic alone) intentionally omit confidence intervals, as they average over heterogeneous and unequally-sized feature sets whose spread reflects between-feature heterogeneity rather than sampling uncertainty of a single estimand. Ranked individual-feature (|d|) values (Table~\ref{tab:cohend_top_features}) are likewise reported as a descriptive screening criterion for candidate selection; formal inferential claims of superiority over baseline models are instead established via the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests in Section~\ref{sec:method_metrics_and_significance}.

Feature-level rankings report the exact \(|d|\) of a single named feature. Because the raw broadband signal contributes three statistics per electrode whereas each frequency band contributes eight, category means rest on unequally sized feature sets, and comparisons between them are indicative rather than exact. Features demonstrating the highest absolute \(|d|\) values were designated as Top Features and isolated for independent predictive baseline modeling.

\subsection{Logistic Regression Modeling and Baselines}
\label{sec:method_baselines_and_logistic_regression}
To benchmark the true predictive capability of the engineered EEG features against chance and clinical baselines, three distinct modeling approaches were evaluated on the temporally isolated test sets (executed via \url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_analysis/step07_train_intra.py} and synthesized in \url{https://github.com/coffeeandcoffee/eeg_tiktok_pipeline/data_analysis/step16_parallel_universes.py}).

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{1. Coinflip Baseline:} A uniform dummy classifier (\texttt{DummyClassifier (strategy='uniform')}) was utilized to establish an empirical chance baseline. Given the rigorous 50/50 balancing of the datasets, this approach reliably generated predictions hovering around the 50\% chance level, verifying that no underlying structural bias existed within the cross-validation folds.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{2. Engagement Index (EI) Baseline:} The pre-calculated EI scalar (derived from \(\beta/(\alpha+\theta)\)) was scaled utilizing a standard scaler (subtracting the mean and scaling to unit variance) and fed as a singular feature into a Logistic Regression model (\verb|LogisticRegression(class_weight='balanced')|). This established a clinical baseline, indicating how well classical engagement theory alone could predict the behavioral swipe decision in this specific short-form video context.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{3. Top Feature Logistic Regression:} To test the predictive power of individual statistical features independently, the features ranked highest via Cohen's \(d\) (e.g., specific band powers or statistical moments) were similarly scaled and evaluated using isolated Logistic Regression models. This isolated approach allows for direct comparison of individual feature efficacy without the confounding complexities of high-dimensional multivariate models.

To ensure a strict one-to-one comparison with the one-dimensional Engagement Index baseline, each macro “Top Parameter” (band, electrode, statistic) was represented by its strongest single constituent feature.

\subsection{Metrics and Significance Testing}
\label{sec:method_metrics_and_significance}
To comprehensively evaluate model performance, standard classification metrics were computed for each cross-validation fold: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and the F\(_1\)-score. In this context, correct prediction of the \texttt{STAY} class reflects successful detection of continuous non-skip viewing, whereas correct prediction of the \texttt{SKIP} class corresponds to identifying the period preceding a swipe-away decision within a 2--5 second pre-action window.

The F\(_1\)-score was used as the primary performance metric, as it balances Precision and Recall and is robust to class imbalance. Metrics were computed separately for each class by treating the respective class as the positive label (i.e., one-vs-rest evaluation for \texttt{SKIP} and \texttt{STAY}).

\textbf{Metric definitions:} Let TP, FP, TN, and FN denote true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, respectively. To achieve optimal classification performance, the correct predictions (TP and TN) must be maximized while the incorrect predictions (FP and FN) are minimized.


\[ \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \]

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \]

\[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \]

\[ F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \]

\vspace{1em}
\textbf{Case 1: \texttt{SKIP} as the positive class}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TP:} correctly predicted \texttt{SKIP} instances
    \item \textbf{TN:} correctly predicted \texttt{STAY} instances
    \item \textbf{FP:} \texttt{STAY} instances incorrectly predicted as \texttt{SKIP}
    \item \textbf{FN:} \texttt{SKIP} instances incorrectly predicted as \texttt{STAY}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{1em}
\textbf{Case 2: \texttt{STAY} as the positive class}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TP:} correctly predicted \texttt{STAY} instances
    \item \textbf{TN:} correctly predicted \texttt{SKIP} instances
    \item \textbf{FP:} \texttt{SKIP} instances incorrectly predicted as \texttt{STAY}
    \item \textbf{FN:} \texttt{STAY} instances incorrectly predicted as \texttt{SKIP}
\end{itemize}
\vspace{1em}

A class-specific F\(_1\)-score substantially exceeding chance level indicates that the model captures discriminative structure beyond random guessing for that class. For reference, under balanced random predictions, the expected F\(_1\)-score is approximately 0.5, whereas under strong class imbalance, this baseline decreases and should be interpreted relative to the class distribution.

To rigorously evaluate whether the Logistic Regression models utilizing individual EEG features outperformed the established baselines across the participant cohort, non-parametric statistical testing was employed. Specifically, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compute the statistical significance (\(p\)-value) of the performance differences between paired model distributions (e.g., Top Feature model vs. EI Baseline model, or EI Baseline model vs. 50\% chance level) at the participant level. For each participant, the performance metric (e.g., F\(_1\)-score) of the evaluated model was contrasted with that of the baseline, yielding 25 paired differences, one per participant.  

The algorithm employs a two-sided Wilcoxon test with a standard significance threshold (\(\alpha = 0.05\)). Because a two-sided test detects significant differences in either direction, a directional validation check is applied post hoc: a model is deemed statistically significantly superior only if two conditions are met simultaneously, namely (i) the test yields \(p < 0.05\), and (ii) the arithmetic mean of the model’s scores across all participants is strictly greater than the arithmetic mean of the corresponding baseline. The rank-biserial correlation (\(r\)) is also computed to quantify the effect size of the Wilcoxon test, indicating the strength of the model's superiority over the compared baseline. This structured chain of statistical proof robustly determines whether individual EEG features can provide a significant improvement over both pure chance and classical clinical engagement metrics when attempting to predict real-world short-form video interactions. Because these comparisons are repeated across a number of feature--baseline pairs, metrics, and evaluation settings, the reported \(p\)-values are not corrected for multiple comparisons; individual comparisons are therefore best read as exploratory screening criteria rather than confirmatory hypothesis tests, with family-wise error control identified as a priority for future replication (see Future Research).

\section{Key Findings from the Experimental Paradigm}
\label{sec:results_exp_learnings}

The experimental paradigm proved feasible in a semi-controlled but still naturalistic setting, and several practical lessons emerged from its execution. Recruitment via email outreach and student mailing lists worked well, especially when paired with a clear and modest incentive. Participants were generally receptive to the study, and most were academically oriented, which appeared to increase interest in the research question and reduce concerns about signing the consent and data-protection forms.

A particularly useful element was the use of iOS Guided Access during both the baseline and TikTok phases. It provided a simple way to constrain the task environment, prevent app switching, and automatically track the duration spent in each app, which matched the temporal structure of the design well. This was especially helpful for the 2-minute baseline and the 25-minute scrolling phase, where reliable timing was essential.

At the same time, the study also highlighted several practical challenges. One recurring difficulty was maintaining a strict and consistent script during participant instruction, especially when explaining what participants should do when the task started, paused, or ended. Another challenge was ensuring that the instructions around synchronization, keypress behavior, and task boundaries were communicated clearly and uniformly across sessions. These issues did not invalidate the experiment, but they do suggest that the protocol would benefit from a more standardized briefing script and a more formalized session flow in future replications.

\section{The Reusable Methodological Pipeline}
\label{sec:results_pipeline}

A major practical contribution of this thesis is the reusable analysis and recording pipeline, which can be accessed through the associated GitHub repository, first mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:method_data_acquisition} and further described in Section~\ref{sec:method_universal_recording_pipeline}. That makes it not only useful for this thesis, but also transferable to other studies with similar naturalistic EEG and behavior-synchronization requirements.

\section{Behavioral Swipe-Burst Phenomenon} \label{sec:results_burst_analysis}

As established in the methodology (Section~\ref{sec:method_behavioral_burst_analysis}), a burst is defined as a sequence of rapidly successive skip events reflecting frantic swiping. To quantify this burst severity, overlapping \texttt{SKIP} windows were grouped into “burst blocks.” The block size $n$ denotes the number of consecutive rapid swipes before a participant pauses to engage. A block of $n=1$ represents standard behavior, whereas larger values (e.g., $n=3$) indicate repeated rapid skipping, possibly without meaningful engagement due to high frequency and action-state inertia.

Figure~\ref{fig:bursts_indiv_3s} shows this behavior at the participant level, revealing a clear dichotomy: some participants exhibit frequent large burst blocks (``fast scrollers'', e.g.\ P17), while others predominantly show $n=1$ behavior (``careful scrollers'', e.g.\ P15). Figure~\ref{fig:bursts_all_3s} aggregates these patterns, confirming that while $n=1$ dominates, a long tail of burst behavior persists.

Finally, Figure~\ref{fig:bursts_all_5s} illustrates the sensitivity to the chosen window size by repeating the analysis with a \(\pm 5.0\)-second window (10.0-second merge threshold). The wider threshold captures a larger portion of the dwell time distribution and merges many isolated swipes into larger burst blocks, substantially reducing the proportion of $n=1$ events, simply by changing the definition of a ``burst block''. Specifically, under the default \(\pm3.0\)s window, 67.4\% of swipe events are isolated singletons (participant-level mean 69.1\% \(\pm\) 16.2\%), while widening to \(\pm5.0\)s reduces this to 48.9\% (participant-level mean 46.9\% \(\pm\) 20.8\%). The large between-participant standard deviation is itself informative: burst tendency appears to be a strongly individual trait rather than a fixed population parameter, foreshadowing the precision-public-health argument developed in Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation}. \input{../05-12_data_analysis_and_results/runs/run_20260611_220844/viz/viz03/burst_stats.tex} This underscores the strong dependence on parameter selection and the need for systematic optimization in future work.

\section{The Engagement Index's Swipe-Prediction Performance}
\label{sec:results_engagement_index}

\subsection{Feature Importance and Neurophysiological Correlates}
\label{sec:results_feature_importance}

In Figure~\ref{fig:cohend_by_freq_band}, we observe that class separation, quantified via Cohen’s \(d\) as described in Section~\ref{sec:method_feature_separation_cohen_d}, is consistently stronger for several individual frequency bands (blue bars) than for the conventional Engagement Index (red dotted line). In particular, the \(\alpha\)-band (8–13 Hz) achieves an effect size approximately 0.005 standard deviations higher than the Engagement Index, and the high-\(\gamma\) band (40–60 Hz), as the strongest candidate, exceeds it by more than 0.03 standard deviations. The Engagement Index — which combines three bands into a single scalar — itself reaches just above 0.02 and is therefore weaker in class separability than any of the individual frequency bands and the raw signal. This suggests that aggregating multiple bands into a single composite metric may dilute discriminative information that is more clearly expressed within specific, well-chosen frequency ranges.

Moreover, class separation reaches values above 0.04 only for a small subset of bands: the raw, broadband signal (without frequency decomposition), the \(\delta\) band (1–4 Hz), the low-\(\gamma\) band (30–40 Hz), and the high-\(\gamma\) band (40–60 Hz). These results indicate that both very low-frequency activity and specific \(\gamma\)-range oscillations carry comparatively strong discriminative structure for the SKIP versus STAY decision. This is consistent with evidence that delta oscillatory responses are involved in decision-making and attentional processes during cognitive tasks \cite{guntekin_review_2016}, and that delta activity is implicated in salience detection, becoming functionally more prominent when higher-frequency, more operationally advanced processing is less dominant \cite{knyazev_eeg_2012} — a pattern structurally consistent with the present finding that band-specific features outperform the composite, higher-frequency-weighted Engagement Index.

Taken together, these findings highlight three key points. First, individual frequency bands exhibit stronger class-separation capability than the conventional Engagement Index, suggesting that analyses based on raw or band-specific signals may be more informative than combined engagement metrics. Second, the raw broadband signal reaches a higher mean \(|d|\) than all separated bands except high \(\gamma\), consistent with prior EEG decoding work showing broadband/undecomposed signal can match or exceed band-limited features depending on task \cite{ovi_frequency_2026}. This comparison should be read with care, since the raw signal is summarized by only three time-domain statistics whereas each band is summarized by eight (Section~\ref{sec:method_feature_engineering}); it nevertheless motivates closer examination of both broadband information and high-\(\gamma\) activity in particular.

P8 Third, the pattern across bands follows a clear up–down–up trend: separability is high at low frequencies, drops to a minimum in the \(\alpha\) range (8–13 Hz), then rises to a global maximum in the high-\(\gamma\) range before decreasing again for very high \(\gamma\) frequencies (60–100 Hz). Non-monotonic, band-specific separability profiles of this kind are not without precedent in EEG classification more broadly; band-wise decoding performance has been shown to peak in non-adjacent frequency ranges rather than scaling smoothly with frequency \cite{plucinska_impact_2022}. This systematic profile offers a frequency-resolved view of where discriminative information is concentrated in the EEG.

\subsection{Engagement Index Model Performance and Benchmark Comparison}
\label{sec:results_model_performance}

The modeling framework described in Section~\ref{sec:method_baselines_and_logistic_regression} was used to evaluate whether the engineered EEG features could predict \texttt{SKIP} versus \texttt{STAY} behavior beyond chance and beyond the conventional Engagement Index. First, a coin-flip baseline established the empirical chance level. Second, the Engagement Index was evaluated as a one-dimensional logistic regression baseline. Third, the strongest features identified in Section~\ref{sec:results_feature_importance} were tested in isolated logistic regression models to assess whether they could improve predictive performance beyond the baseline models.

To evaluate predictive performance, standard classification metrics were computed as described in Section~\ref{sec:method_metrics_and_significance}. In particular, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and the F\(_1\)-score were calculated for each cross-validation fold, separately for both classes via one-vs-rest evaluation. The F\(_1\)-score served as the primary metric because it balances Precision and Recall and is therefore more informative than Accuracy under the present class structure.

Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as outlined in Section~\ref{sec:method_metrics_and_significance}. The coin-flip baseline serves as the reference for chance-level performance, whereas the Engagement Index is used as the clinical baseline for subsequent comparisons. Performance is reported both for the intra-subject evaluation setting, in which models were trained and tested within the same participant, and for the inter-subject evaluation setting, in which models were generalized across participants. The corresponding results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:metrics_1_intra} and Table~\ref{tab:metrics_1_inter}, respectively. In these tables, green values indicate metrics that are both significantly different from the baseline (\(p < 0.05\)) and higher than the baseline on average, thus reflecting genuine performance gains rather than mere statistical deviation.

At the inter-subject level shown in Table~\ref{tab:metrics_1_inter}, the Engagement Index logistic regression yields a statistically significant improvement over the coin-flip baseline only for \texttt{STAY} as the positive class. The most prominent effect is Recall, which rises to 89.2\% compared with 51.7\% for the coin-flip baseline. This indicates that the model is highly sensitive to \texttt{STAY} windows and rarely misses them, but it does not necessarily imply balanced or reliable class discrimination on its own. In fact, high Recall in combination with only moderate F\(_1\)-scores suggests a strong tendency to capture the majority of \texttt{STAY} instances while still leaving room for false positives, meaning that the model may be better at recognizing \texttt{STAY} periods than at sharply distinguishing them from \texttt{SKIP}.

The corresponding F\(_1\)-scores provide a more nuanced interpretation. At 62.0\% for training and 62.5\% for testing, the model performs above chance and remains relatively stable across folds, which argues against severe overfitting in the inter-subject setting. However, the gain over the coin-flip baseline is modest in absolute terms, especially when viewed alongside the comparatively large improvement in Recall. This combination suggests that the Engagement Index carries some predictive structure, but that this structure is unevenly distributed across metrics: it may identify \texttt{STAY} behavior reasonably well, yet it does not provide a uniformly strong decision boundary between the two classes.

Importantly, these results also clarify what the Engagement Index can and cannot do in this dataset. It appears capable of capturing a limited but real signal related to the \texttt{STAY} class specifically, but it is not sufficient as a standalone descriptor of the full SKIP-versus-STAY decision. The fact that the model improves clearly in Recall while remaining only moderately strong in F\(_1\)-score raises a central interpretive question: does the Engagement Index reflect a broad engagement-related tendency, or is it primarily sensitive to a subset of windows that are easier to classify as \texttt{STAY}? This ambiguity motivates the next analytical step in Section \ref{sec:results_alternative_index}, namely to examine which specific features, electrodes, and frequency ranges contribute more directly to class separability and whether they offer a more interpretable explanation as an ``Alternative Index'' over the aggregate Engagement Index. In that sense, the present numbers do not merely indicate performance; they identify the precise limitation of the baseline and justify moving toward feature-level interpretation and model comparison.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:results_interpretation}

The results above do not yet justify strong causal claims about underlying brain mechanisms, but they do point to several robust interpretive directions. Most importantly, the analyses suggest that the conventional Engagement Index is not wrong in a trivial sense; rather, it appears to capture a limited and asymmetrical subset of the behaviorally relevant signal. In particular, it seems more aligned with sustained \texttt{STAY}-like states than with the more transient, decision-like \texttt{SKIP} events. This asymmetry is crucial, because it implies that the Engagement Index may reflect a broad state of ongoing engagement, but not the full cognitive transition that leads to disengagement. From a scientific perspective, this is a more useful and humble conclusion than simply saying that the index "works" or "does not work": it works partly, in a constrained way, and its failure modes are themselves informative.

A second important point is that the strongest features are not distributed uniformly across the EEG. Instead, they cluster in specific electrodes, frequency bands, and feature types, with raw broadband variability at TP10, high-\(\gamma\) activity at AF7, and delta peak frequency at AF8 repeatedly emerging as informative. This pattern suggests that the relevant signal may not be a single abstract ``engagement'' quantity, but rather a combination of lateralized, frequency-specific, and task-sensitive components. The fact that raw broadband signal sometimes outperforms band-limited features also raises the possibility that the discriminative information is not fully captured by conventional band definitions alone. At the same time, this may also indicate sensitivity to non-neural factors, including motion-related or muscle-related artifacts, which must be treated cautiously rather than ignored.

\subsection{Neuroscience}
From a neuroscientific perspective, the most consistent result is that separability increases when the signal is described more specifically, especially in the raw broadband TP10 feature and in AF7 high-\(\gamma\) activity. This may indicate that the decision to continue scrolling versus disengage is associated with a distributed and frequency-sensitive pattern rather than with a single canonical engagement rhythm. The repeated prominence of high-\(\gamma\) and delta-derived features is notable because these ranges are often linked to sharply different physiological processes; however, the present data do not allow a definitive mapping from feature to neural mechanism. What can be said more cautiously is that the observed pattern is compatible with the idea that the task engages both rapid sensorimotor dynamics and higher-order control or monitoring processes, with anterior frontal and temporal sites contributing differently to the observed class structure.

At the same time, the broadband superiority of TP10 and the strong performance of AF7 in high-frequency ranges create an important interpretive friction point. If the signal were purely reflecting a clean cortical engagement index, one might expect a more stable advantage for the conventional composite metric. Instead, the data imply that narrower and more local signal components contain more class-relevant information. This opens at least two competing hypotheses: either the task truly recruits region-specific neurophysiological signatures that the Engagement Index averages away, or the apparent gain is partly driven by peripheral contamination, such as facial muscle activity, motion, or device-dependent noise.  

This pattern is consistent with established evidence that temporal electrodes are disproportionately susceptible to EMG contamination from the underlying temporalis muscle, which persists even at rest and is not fully removed by standard ICA-based artifact correction \cite{goncharova_emg_2003, yilmaz_facial_2019, mcmenamin_electromyogenic_2011}; because reported EMG beta-peak power extends as low as 13~Hz at frontal and temporal sites \cite{goncharova_emg_2003}, this ambiguity is not confined to the high-gamma and broadband features discussed above, and plausibly extends to alpha- and beta-band features at these same electrodes. Because two independent Muse S device units were used across participants (Section~\ref{sec:method_data_acquisition}), device-dependent differences in electrode contact or impedance could further modulate this susceptibility, compounding rather than resolving the ambiguity between neural and myogenic origin \cite{whitham_scalp_2007}.

A related and separate source of ambiguity concerns the decision to report all results on un-notched data (Section~\ref{sec:method_universal_preprocessing_pipeline}). Because the 50~Hz mains frequency falls inside the high-gamma band, and because low-cost dry-electrode amplifiers are additionally susceptible to mains-driven intermodulation artifacts flanking the line frequency \cite{pattisapu_stimulus-induced_2021}, any high-gamma or broadband finding reported here (e.g.\ AF7 high-gamma mean, TP10 raw standard deviation) cannot be fully dissociated from possible line-noise contamination. This is an acknowledged limitation of the present analysis rather than a validated non-issue: a targeted notch-versus-no-notch ablation on the reported top features is left to future work.

The delta result is consistent with a broader literature linking delta oscillatory responses to decision-making and attentional processing in awake cognitive paradigms, with reduced delta responses treated as a candidate marker of impaired cognitive processing \cite{guntekin_review_2016}. From an evolutionary-functional perspective, delta oscillations have also been proposed to index salience detection and basic attentional/motivational processing, rising in relative prominence precisely when higher-frequency, more operationally flexible systems are less engaged or less effective \cite{knyazev_eeg_2012} — a pattern plausibly relevant here given the Engagement Index's weak SKIP-detection performance despite relying primarily on beta, alpha, and theta power. This literature has not previously been applied to short-form video engagement classification specifically; the only directly comparable prior evidence comes from studies with substantially different scope: an adolescent (mean age 12.3) SVA sample with a small, borderline-significant delta--executive-function link (F=2.184, p=.040) \cite{xu_neural_2024}, and a smaller (n=10), coarser binary phone-use classifier that found beta and gamma, rather than delta, as strongest predictors using different gamma-band boundaries \cite{rahman_detection_2025}. Neither study addresses a self-paced skip decision, underscoring that the present question is novel rather than merely under-replicated. The present AF8 delta peak-frequency finding is best read as consistent with delta's established general role in attention and salience, rather than as a validated TikTok-specific marker.

The electrode pattern also raises a lateralization question that cannot yet be answered decisively. AF7 and TP10 appear more informative than AF8 and TP9, which may point to a functional asymmetry in how engagement-related and action-related states are expressed during naturalistic scrolling. However, because two physical units of the headband were used and because broadband effects may partially reflect artifact structure, this should be interpreted as a hypothesis for future work rather than as a confirmed hemispheric specialization. In the next step, a targeted analysis of artifact-sensitive channels, spectral decomposition, and device-specific effects would be needed to determine whether these patterns reflect genuine neurophysiology or measurement confounds.

\subsection{Psychology}
Psychologically, the results suggest that the act of skipping a TikTok video is not simply the inverse of being engaged. The Engagement Index performs better for \texttt{STAY} than for \texttt{SKIP}, which implies that sustained attention or passive continuation may be easier to detect than the brief decision point at which a participant chooses to move on. This distinction matters because it suggests that \texttt{SKIP} is not merely a low-engagement state, but may represent a qualitatively different behavioral event: an active choice, a threshold crossing, or a moment of disengagement that is preceded by a narrower and more transient signal profile. The stronger performance of features such as AF8 delta peak frequency and TP10 raw standard deviation supports the idea that the decisive moment may be better captured by fine-grained temporal structure than by a broad engagement aggregate.

The burst analysis adds an additional psychological layer. Some participants behave as ``fast scrollers'' with repeated burst blocks, whereas others show mostly isolated swipes. This heterogeneity implies that the psychological process underlying scrolling is not uniform across participants. Some users may evaluate content quickly and repeatedly, while others may linger, reflect, or maintain a more stable engagement rhythm. That individual variability likely explains why per-participant and cross-participant performance differ so strongly, and why the same feature may appear informative in one setting but unstable in another.

The modest but consistent success of individual features over the Engagement Index also suggests that a psychologically meaningful model of scrolling may need to distinguish between sustained engagement, momentary decision pressure, and motor execution. The present results are compatible with such a layered interpretation, but they do not yet prove it. What they do show is that an aggregate engagement score is too coarse to explain the full behavioral variance, especially when the behavior is embedded in a rapid, naturalistic, and self-paced interaction environment.

\subsection{Behaviour}
From a behavioral standpoint, the strongest conclusion is that the \texttt{SKIP}/\texttt{STAY} decision is heterogeneous and temporally structured. The burst analysis shows that many swipes occur in clusters rather than as isolated events, which means that the observed behavior is not simply a sequence of independent binary decisions. Instead, it appears to reflect a mixture of cautious sampling, rapid dismissal, and occasional repeated disengagement. This helps explain why feature-level models can outperform the Engagement Index in some cases: the neural signal may be tracking not only whether a participant is engaged, but also where they are in a short behavioral sequence leading toward or away from a swipe.

The fact that raw TP10 variability and AF8 delta peak frequency improve \texttt{SKIP} detection suggests that the behavioral decision to move on may leave a measurable trace in the EEG, but only weakly and only under certain feature definitions. The most defensible interpretation is therefore not that the model ``predicts choice'' in a strong sense, but that it detects behavioral precursors or correlates of the \texttt{SKIP} decision with limited but meaningful sensitivity. That is an important distinction for the thesis: the signal is informative, but it is not deterministic.

The results also show that behavior is strongly shaped by parameter choices, especially in the burst analysis. Changing the window definition changes how events are grouped, and changing the feature definition changes which patterns emerge as separable. This dependency implies that future behavioral analyses should not rely on a single segmentation or metric, but rather test whether the observed structure is stable across multiple reasonable parameterizations.

\subsection{Application}
In applied terms, these findings are best understood as a proof of concept for more transparent and behaviorally grounded engagement modeling. Rather than demonstrating a ready-to-deploy predictive system, the results show that naturalistic scrolling behavior can be measured with sufficient structure to support interpretable feature-level analysis, and that some EEG-derived features outperform the conventional Engagement Index in ways that are meaningful for both explanation and future application.

The practical relevance of these findings lies less in the idea of a fully deployable EEG-based prediction system and more in the methodological and conceptual direction they provide. The present results show that naturalistic scrolling behavior can be measured, segmented, and partially explained using a compact set of EEG-derived features, and that these features can outperform a conventional aggregate index in ways that are interpretable at the level of signal structure. This matters because it suggests a path toward more transparent models of engagement: rather than relying on a single composite score, future systems may be built from a small number of physiologically meaningful components that are easier to interpret, debug, and adapt.

One immediate application is in the optimization of message delivery in health communication and behavior-change interventions. If certain EEG signatures are more associated with continued attention or imminent disengagement, then intervention content could be structured to better match those moments of attentional stability or vulnerability. In principle, this could help improve the timing, length, or sequencing of health messages, public-health prompts, educational micro-content, or persuasive interventions in digital environments. For example, short-form health information could be adapted to identify when a user is likely to continue engaging versus when they are likely to skip, which could inform both content design and delivery timing -- though at present this discrimination remains modest and, in the cross-participant setting, does not yet exceed the chance-equivalent F\(_1\) reference established in Section~\ref{sec:results_model_performance}.  

A second application is in the development of explainable and lightweight screening tools for user engagement in naturalistic settings. Because the strongest results here emerged from a small number of features rather than a large black-box model, the present work may support the design of interpretable real-time monitors that are more feasible than complex deep-learning approaches. Such monitors could be useful in user-testing environments, educational research, human-computer interaction, or cognitive ergonomics, where the goal is not necessarily to make a diagnostic prediction but to identify when a stimulus fails to hold attention or when a participant shifts into a disengaged state.

At the same time, the present results also define the limits of immediate application. The predictive performance is not yet strong enough to justify high-stakes individualized deployment, and the possibility of artifact-related contributions means that any translational use would require substantial validation, device standardization, and robustness testing. In particular, the strong effects observed at TP10 and AF7 raise the possibility that some predictive power may arise from non-neural sources such as jaw movement, facial tension, or other task-correlated artifacts -- a risk that, per Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation}, plausibly extends into alpha- and beta-band features at these electrodes and not only into the high-gamma and broadband features discussed above. For this reason, the present study is best interpreted as a proof of principle for explainable signal extraction rather than as a finished applied system.  

\subsubsection{Recommendations for Policy Makers}
Three concrete regulatory implications follow from the present findings without requiring any claim beyond what the data and the cited literature support.

First, \textbf{watch time is the wrong regulatory quantity}. Both platform self-reporting and emerging digital-services regulation frequently treat viewing duration as a proxy for user interest or content value. The present results, together with the behavioral literature, indicate that continuous non-skip viewing is equally consistent with dissociative, non-encoding consumption as with genuine engagement \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026}; a metric that cannot distinguish the two should not be used as evidence of user benefit in transparency reports or audit frameworks. Policy makers evaluating platform disclosures should therefore require engagement measures that are validated against independent behavioral or physiological criteria rather than dwell time alone. This recommendation follows from the cited dissociation and flow literature \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026} rather than from the present EEG results directly, which characterize a candidate physiological signature of the pre-swipe state but do not themselves measure dissociation, flow, or recall.

Second, \textbf{friction is an evidence-backed lever}. Interface features that segment or interrupt continuous scrolling --- custom lists and reading-history labels, in particular --- have been shown to significantly reduce self-reported dissociation without removing content \cite{baughan_i_2022} (a single study, on a Twitter client rather than short-form video; replication on TikTok-like platforms is needed before generalizing). Mandating or incentivizing such segmentation cues in feed-based products is a realistic, minimally invasive intervention whose mechanism is already empirically characterized, in contrast to blunt instruments such as age-gating or time caps.

Third, \textbf{auditability of engagement optimization}. The present thesis demonstrates that the neural precursors of the skip decision are partially measurable with consumer-grade hardware. Platforms plausibly have access to behavioral, and possibly physiological, signals at a scale and precision this thesis cannot match; if so, the same auditability logic applies with greater force. This thesis has no access to platform-internal data, and this claim should be read as a reasonable extrapolation rather than a demonstrated fact. Regulators should treat engagement-optimization systems as auditable algorithmic systems and require disclosure of the behavioral targets they optimize, precisely because the optimized quantity (continued viewing) is, per the above, not a wellbeing proxy.

\subsubsection{Recommendations for Digital Global Public Health Research}
For digital global public health --- including the research agenda of the HPI Chair for Digital Global Public Health, whose stated priorities include disease prevention, evidence-based health communication, and precision public health --- the present work suggests three tangible extensions.

First, \textbf{message pre-testing panels}: the full recording and analysis pipeline of this thesis runs on a consumer headset, a laptop, and open-source software, and is reproducible by an independent researcher (Section~\ref{sec:results_pipeline}). This makes small-$n$ physiological pre-testing of health messages --- comparing candidate formats of, e.g., a vaccination or antimicrobial-resistance communication for their pre-skip signature before campaign deployment --- more accessible to public institutions than fully custom or clinical-grade EEG pipelines would be, analogous to the message pre-testing already standard in commercial advertising but at research-grade transparency -- though, as Section~\ref{sec:results_pipeline} notes, substantial engineering investment was still required to reach this point, and this should be read as a reduction in barrier-to-entry rather than a turnkey solution.

Second, \textbf{attentional reach as a campaign endpoint}: public health communication on short-form platforms is known to underperform entertainment content in engagement metrics, with institutional accounts reaching orders-of-magnitude fewer users than individual creators \cite{mccashin_using_2023}. Campaign evaluation should therefore report not only impressions but survival within the feed --- the probability of a health message being skipped within its first seconds --- a quantity the present labeling framework provides an initial, currently modest-power operationalization of (Section~\ref{sec:results_model_performance}).

Third, \textbf{precision public health applied to attention}: in the same way precision public health stratifies prevention by risk profile, the inter-individual heterogeneity observed here (``frantic'' versus ``careful'' scrollers, Section~\ref{sec:results_burst_analysis}; the failure of cross-participant models relative to within-participant models) implies that attentional response to health content is itself a stratifiable trait, and that one-size-fits-all message design leaves systematic reach on the table.

\subsubsection{Recommendations for Health Message Design}
At the level of individual message creation, the findings translate into concrete design guidance.

(i) \textbf{The decision plausibly precedes the swipe}: the \texttt{SKIP}-predictive window examined here (2--5 seconds before the physical action) was adopted as a literature-motivated heuristic starting point rather than an independently validated decision boundary (Section~\ref{sec:op_labeling_params}), and class separability within it, while statistically distinguishable from the Engagement Index baseline, remains modest in absolute terms (Section~\ref{sec:results_model_performance}). With that scoping in mind, the general pattern -- that some pre-swipe neural signature precedes the physical swipe action -- is broadly consistent with content needing to establish interest early rather than relying on a delayed payoff; testing where within a video's opening seconds this signature is strongest, and whether it maps onto specific content features, is a natural extension of the present labeling framework rather than a claim established by it here.

(ii) \textbf{Design for the dissociated viewer}: because a substantial fraction of viewing occurs in a passive, low-encoding state \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026}, message formats should include pattern interrupts --- changes in pacing, framing, or sensory structure --- intended to manufacture re-engagement points rather than rely on spontaneous recovery of attention, since Baughan et al.\ found such recovery is often only recognized retrospectively, if at all \cite{baughan_i_2022}.

(iii) \textbf{Do not optimize health content for dwell time}: a message that is watched to completion in a dissociative state may be encoded worse than a shorter message that triggers active appraisal; evaluation should weight recall and behavior change over watch-through rate.

(iv) \textbf{Respect burst structure}: swipes cluster into rapid bursts for a substantial share of events, though the exact proportion is parameter-dependent -- 67.4\% of swipes are isolated singletons under the default \(\pm3.0\)s window, falling to 48.9\% under a wider \(\pm5.0\)s window (Section~\ref{sec:results_burst_analysis}) -- and burst tendency varies sharply between participants (SD of 16--21 percentage points around these means), consistent with the individual heterogeneity noted in Section~\ref{sec:results_interpretation}. This favors repeated, distributed placement over single high-cost placements as a population-level default, while flagging burst-aware, per-user delivery timing as a natural extension once outcome-linked validation is available; the present work establishes that swipes cluster, not that burst membership itself predicts weaker message encoding or evaluation.

\subsubsection{Societal Scope: An Explicitly Speculative Outlook}
The following paragraph is a deliberate change of register: it is a hypothesis-generating extrapolation, not a result of this thesis, and is marked as such. What the data of this thesis and the cited literature jointly establish is narrow but non-trivial: short-form feeds occupy users in a high-frequency stream of appraisal micro-decisions; a substantial share of the resulting viewing time is spent in states of reduced self-awareness and poor encoding \cite{baughan_i_2022, knierim_flow_2026}; and attention is a finite, fluctuating resource whose allocation determines what is subsequently remembered \cite{debettencourt_sustained_2021}. From these premises, a \emph{time-displacement hypothesis} follows naturally, though it is not tested here: hours spent in dissociative feed consumption are hours of attentional and social bandwidth not spent elsewhere --- including on the effortful, in-person interactions through which relationships are formed. Whether this displacement contributes causally to widely discussed, though contested, population-level trends in youth socialization, loneliness, or, further downstream, family formation is precisely the kind of long-range causal chain the present study can neither support nor refute, and this thesis makes no such claim. What it can state is the conditional: \emph{if} such displacement effects exist, then the mechanisms studied here --- the moment of attentional lock-in, the dissociative default, the decodable pre-skip state --- are the proximate levers through which they operate, and the same measurement framework used here to describe them is the framework with which mitigation (segmentation cues, adaptive interruption, attention-aware content policy) would be evaluated. Quantifying the displacement chain, and testing realistic mitigations against it, is left to future research; the contribution of the present work is to make its first link measurable.

Finally, a dual-use note is warranted: any marker that helps health communicators survive the feed helps commercial and political actors equally. The appropriate response is not to withhold such research --- platforms already possess strictly superior behavioral data --- but to conduct it transparently, under ethics oversight (Section~\ref{sec:method_participants}), and to pair it with the auditability recommendations above.

\section{Future Research}
Future research should first focus on validation and replication. The present findings are promising, but they are based on a single dataset with a relatively small sample size and two physical units of the same recording device. Replication in an independent sample is therefore essential, ideally with more uniform hardware, tighter artifact control, a broader and less academically-skewed participant pool, and multiple-comparison-corrected significance testing across the feature--baseline comparisons reported here. A key priority should be to test whether the same features -- especially TP10 raw standard deviation, AF7 high-\(\gamma\) mean, and AF8 delta peak frequency -- remain informative under repeated acquisition, different content types, and different user populations.

A second major direction is mechanistic clarification. The present results leave open whether the strongest effects reflect genuinely neural engagement processes, movement-related artifacts, or a mixture of both. Future work should therefore include explicit artifact analyses, such as electromyographic control across the alpha-through-gamma range (not only high-gamma or broadband), motion monitoring, ICA-based comparison, channel-quality assessments, and device-specific sensitivity checks. It would also be valuable to compare broadband features against strictly cleaned, source-reconstructed, or artifact-masked data to determine how much of the predictive signal survives after conservative preprocessing.

\subsection{1. The Research Question and Narrative Arc}
\textbf{The core question:} Can consumer-grade EEG (Muse S) accurately identify periods of sustained cognitive engagement (\texttt{STAY} state) versus imminent task abandonment (\texttt{SKIP} state) during naturalistic TikTok browsing?

To answer this honestly and logically, the academic story arc must flow step-by-step from established clinical baselines to our customized, high-dimensional machine learning architecture. We must prove that traditional metrics fail to capture the rapid micro-decisions of short-form media, and that a structurally broader mapping of the brain's frequencies (specifically high-frequency bands) is required to predict sustained engagement.

\subsection{2. Step 1: The Clinical Baseline (Engagement Index)}
\textbf{Logical Step:} Before building complex models, we must test whether established neuroscience equations can answer our question. The traditional Engagement Index ($EI = \beta / (\alpha + \theta)$) was designed for sustained attention tasks. Since it relies on lower frequency bands ($<30$~Hz), it safely ignores $>50$~Hz noise.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What the data shows:} Our 5-fold cross-validated Logistic Regression models trained on EI ratios achieved an aggregate mean accuracy of \textbf{52.3\% $\pm$ 6.2\%} and a mean \texttt{STAY} Recall of \textbf{52.8\%} across 25 participants. Only 4 of 25 participants showed a statistically significant difference in EI between STAY and SKIP states ($p<0.05$). [Source: \verb|ei_corrected_20260506_161647/ei_aggregate_summary.csv|]

    \item \textbf{Narrative Conclusion:} The standard clinical formula fundamentally fails to reliably discriminate short-form video micro-decisions better than random chance. The neural signature for rapidly maintaining or dropping engagement on TikTok does not map cleanly to simple $\beta / (\alpha + \theta)$ ratios. This is consistent with Apicella et al., who found that EI-based classification in a controlled learning engagement task yielded accuracy performances that were ``not optimal,'' explicitly noting the index is ``mainly used in non-predictive applications'' \cite{apicella_eeg-based_2022}. We capture the geometric variance (mean, std, min, max) of all 7 frequency bands across all 4 electrodes (112 features) and feed it into a Random Forest ensemble. We apply a standard 50~Hz Notch Filter to rigorously clean the data of electrical power-line noise, as is standard practice.

    \item \textbf{What the data shows (CORRECTED --- leakage-free evaluation):} Using blocked temporal 5-fold CV with 3-second gaps between blocks (train: 80\% overlap, test: 0\% overlap), the RF-112 models achieved a mean intra-subject accuracy of \textbf{53.8\% $\pm$ 7.2\%} with 20 of 25 participants above the 50\% chance baseline (Wilcoxon $p=0.0015$ vs.\ chance). The model can detect sustained cognitive engagement significantly better than chance ($p=0.0015$), establishing that the structural variance of the EEG signal holds predictive value for micro-decisions within a proof-of-concept scope. \subsection{4. Step 3: The High-Frequency Hypothesis (RF-112 \textit{WITHOUT} Notch Filter)}

Our Notch filter from Step 2 potentially obliterated this vital neural data by aggressively cutting at 50~Hz. The finding is consistent with Pattisapu and Ray's observation that low-cost EEG amplifiers can detect stimulus-induced gamma oscillations \cite{pattisapu_stimulus-induced_2021}.

\subsection{5. Step 4: Cross-Participant Generalizability (LOGO-CV)}
\label{sec:logo_cv}
\textbf{Logical Step:} Having found a signature within individuals, we must honestly test if this signature is a universal human trait. Can a model trained on 24 people diagnose the engagement of a completely unseen 25th person?
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What the data shows:} The Leave-One-Group-Out Cross-Validation resulted in an aggregate mean accuracy of \textbf{49.2\%} (random chance). While mean recall appeared high (68.6\%), the collapsed accuracy proves the models resorted to biased guessing rather than recognizing a universal pattern.
    \item \textbf{Narrative Conclusion:} The hypothesis of a universal signature is disproved. The cortical state variations defining active TikTok engagement and disengagement are fundamentally \textit{idiosyncratic}. This result is not anomalous: it is consistent with a prediction embedded in the foundational naturalistic neuroscience literature. Hasson et al.\ demonstrated that while primary sensory cortices synchronize reliably across individuals during naturalistic viewing, higher-order association areas---including the supramarginal gyrus, angular gyrus, and prefrontal cortex---consistently \textit{fail} to show intersubject coherence, precisely because these regions are governed by ``unique, individual variations'' rather than stereotyped stimulus-driven responses \cite{hasson_intersubject_2004}. The engagement and disengagement states targeted by the present study are mediated by exactly this class of higher-order, prefrontal executive processing. The inferential step from that finding to the present result is the following: if the cortical regions encoding engagement states exhibit fundamentally idiosyncratic activation profiles across individuals even during passive naturalistic viewing, then a supervised classifier trained on spectral features derived from those regions across 24 subjects cannot be expected to learn a universal decision boundary that transfers to a 25th. The LOGO-CV collapse to 49.2\% accuracy is therefore a theoretically expected outcome, not a methodological failure. This inter-subject variability in EEG-based cognitive state classification is a well-documented challenge: Apicella et al.\ explicitly characterize the non-stationarity of EEG signals as producing greater data variability between subjects, making cross-subject classification ``a very challenging task,'' even under controlled laboratory conditions with standardized stimuli \cite{apicella_eeg-based_2022}. The present study's naturalistic paradigm, which introduces additional variability through individualized feed content and unconstrained motor behavior, renders this challenge structurally more severe. Bellos et al.\ further identify inter-subject variability as a persistent open problem across the EEG-based user engagement literature \cite{bellos_methods_2025}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6. Step 5: Behavioral Heterogeneity (Skip Bias)}
\textbf{Logical Step:} We must humbly analyze what our model is actually predicting. Is the \texttt{SKIP} class a pure, isolated decision that a single video is boring, or does it contain noise?
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What the data shows:} Analysis of the continuous target blocks revealed that while the mode skip behavior is an isolated swipe ($>3$ seconds of viewing), there are significant chains of rapid, consecutive skips ($<3$ seconds of viewing).
    \item \textbf{Narrative Conclusion:} The \texttt{SKIP} state is cognitively heterogeneous. It captures both genuine content-locked appraisal failures and a generalized "disengaged browsing" attentional momentum. The predictive neural signature fundamentally contrasts active sustained focus against this mixed disengaged background state.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{7. Step 6: Universal SFEI Extraction and Battle-Test (The Final Constraint)}
\textbf{Logical Step:} While the 112-feature Random Forest model works, it is a complex "black box". The holy grail of BCI research is a simple, universal mathematical equation (like the traditional EI: $\beta / (\alpha + \theta)$). To attempt this, we trained surrogate decision trees across all 25 participants to find the "Universal Root Dimensions" driving the predictions. We found that frontal High Gamma ($\text{AF8\_high\_gamma\_mean}$) and frontal Delta ($\text{AF7\_delta\_min}$) were the most common primary splitting features. Based on this, we proposed a Short-Form Engagement Index (SFEI): $SFEI = \text{AF8\_high\_gamma\_mean} / \text{AF7\_delta\_min}$. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What the data shows:} We battle-tested this new $SFEI$ formula against the traditional $EI$ using identical 5-fold cross-validation. The $SFEI$ achieved a mean accuracy of \textbf{51.6\%} and a recall of \textbf{52.2\%}. Both the traditional $EI$ (52.3\%) and the proposed $SFEI$ (51.6\%) failed to reliably beat random chance across the population. [Source: \verb|sfei_battletest_20260424_230531/sfei_metrics.json|]
    \item \textbf{Narrative Conclusion:} This confirms the constraint of consumer BCI in dynamic environments. Reducing algorithmic media engagement to a static, universal equation is insufficient. The cognitive state is too complex and idiosyncratic. High-dimensional, individualized machine learning approaches (like the RF-112 model achieving 53.8\% via blocked temporal CV, $p=0.0015$) are required. The failure of the universal SFEI is consistent with the LOGO-CV collapse: the engagement signature is fundamentally individualized.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Methodology for Significance Testing}
To rigorously determine whether a predictive model meaningfully outperforms a random baseline (Coin Flip) or a standard reference (Engagement Index, EI LR), this pipeline employs the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Standard parametric tests assume that the performance metrics across participants are normally distributed. In small-sample physiological data (N=25 participants), this assumption is often violated. Performance is evaluated using paired metrics from the 25 individual cross-validation folds. The test calculates the difference in performance between the evaluated model and the baseline model for each participant. The algorithm employs a two-sided Wilcoxon test with a standard significance threshold ($\alpha = 0.05$). To strictly guarantee superiority, a directional check is applied post-hoc: the arithmetic mean of the model's scores across all participants must be strictly greater than the arithmetic mean of the baseline.

\subsection{Outlook and Implications for Future BCI Design}
A critical implication of this thesis is the rejection of the ``Universal BCI Equation'' paradigm. The LOGO-CV validation directly demonstrates this: the Random Forest---a non-linear, 112-component model---completely fails to generalize across participants (49.2\% accuracy), providing evidence that the bottleneck is biological idiosyncrasy, not mathematical complexity. Future development of consumer BCI systems for algorithmic media interaction should pivot toward personalized, high-dimensional machine learning calibration architectures. Additionally, this thesis demonstrates that standard overlapping-window evaluation in BCI research can inflate reported performance by $\sim$8 percentage points, highlighting the importance of temporally-separated evaluation protocols in future work.
