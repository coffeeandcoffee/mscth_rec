The paragraph was written from inside the writer's head, not for the reader. I'd call it the **curse of knowledge**: the writer knew what every compressed phrase meant and forgot the reader doesn't.

That showed up in five ways:

1. **Shorthand with no explanation.** "Not deployable", "proxy", "conjecture", "indicator" and "construct" are labels. Each one stood in for an idea the reader never got to see.
2. **Words pointing at nothing.** "It", "this", "the bridge", "the third" and "the definition above" all assumed the reader still held something from paragraphs or sections earlier.
3. **Slogans instead of sentences.** "A proxy measures the proxy", "most settings have no thumb" and "survives contact with" were written to sound memorable, not to be understood. They reward the writer and make the reader decode.
4. **Too much confidence.** "None of that survives" and "the two properties the results identify as definitional" claimed more than one study of 25 people can support.
5. **A factual error hidden in the density.** "Without which performance fell to chance" didn't match the Results: even with calibration, the models were only slightly above chance. Dense wording makes an error like that easy to miss, for the writer and for the examiner.

In short, the paragraph showed off its understanding instead of passing it on. The fix each time was to name the concrete thing, give an everyday example or analogy where the idea was abstract, and check each claim against the Results."
